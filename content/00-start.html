Put a strip of tape on a wheel. Send one letter from your laptop. The tape moves, then stops. That tiny event contains most of a robot: a command, some code, a power source, a mechanism, and a result you can check. We’re going to make that happen, then add one useful trick at a time.

You bring more than fifteen years of professional programming and an existing agentic workflow. I’ll treat software development as a tool you already own. We’ll spend our learning time on the new interfaces: a printed fit, a power supply under load, a joint’s torque, measurements you can trust, and a model whose performance holds up on data it did not train on. Your early printing, Arduino, and Fusion experience is enough to begin.

Here’s the deal

I’ll make the first round of design decisions: which board, which motor driver, what to print, what command to send, and what counts as working. You’ll do the part that turns instructions into skill: predict one result, build it, and look closely at what happened. Later, we’ll deliberately change a decision and see what it buys us.

Put these tools beside the tray

  • A laptop, Arduino IDE 2, your slicer, and Fusion. Python arrives in Chapter 06.
  • A ruler, digital calipers if you have them, small Phillips and flat screwdrivers, flush cutters, and wire strippers.
  • A basic multimeter with DC-voltage mode and insulated clip leads/adapters that fit its probes. We’ll start by measuring battery voltage, with the leads in COM and V.
  • A temperature-controlled soldering iron, stand, electronics solder, a small scrap of perfboard for practice, electrical insulating tape, eye protection, and ventilation for the shield headers. If you have never soldered, use the guided practice step in Chapter 02 or have a makerspace helper fit the headers before proceeding.
  • A sturdy box that holds the deck with both wheels clear of the table. This becomes the robot’s first test stand.

No Raspberry Pi, GPU, LiPo pack, ROS installation, or spider kit is required for the main route. The simulation chapters run on the laptop and do not connect to the robot.

When attention wanders, leave a breadcrumb

At the end of a session, write three short lines in the notebook below: Last thing that worked. What I saw. Next physical action. “Open Chapter 03 and upload rover.ino with the battery off” is a useful restart note. “Learn electronics” will send you browsing.

A session can end after one fit test. Take a photo, switch off the motor power, unplug USB, and leave the part with its label. You don’t owe the book a streak or a catch-up weekend. The next step will still be here.

Give your agents an experiment contract

Use your existing agentic workflow for scaffolding, plotting, CAD scripts, and code review. Before a hardware change, specify the exact parts, units, command bounds, startup state, timeout behavior, and observation that would support your prediction. Ask an agent to implement the smallest change and list the assumptions it could not verify. Keep the physical measurement and acceptance decision with you.

Using a coding agent and training a model are different skills. In the first-model plan, you will choose examples and targets, fit parameters from training data, and evaluate on reserved data. Agents can write the collector and training loop; you will decide whether the experiment actually demonstrates learning.

Where this leads: “forward” becomes coordinated joints

Today, a command chooses wheel directions. Later, a controller can turn a requested forward speed and turn rate into joint targets. Sensors report what the body did; limits and timeouts remain close to the motors. Chapters 09–10 first give you an executable simulation and controller search, then a plan for training a small predictive model. A learned locomotion policy comes after those experiments and the physical behavior are understood well enough to compare.
