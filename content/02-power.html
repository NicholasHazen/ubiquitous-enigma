02 · Bring the bench to life

Wire the wheel motors

One board makes decisions. Another switches motor power. We will connect them without turning the bench into a guessing game.

Put your unpowered rolling chassis beside the electronics. It is beginning to look like a robot in pieces, which is a perfectly respectable species of robot. Today we will give those pieces a power path. The wheels will stay still until we have loaded our own program in the next chapter.

On the bench · about 45 minutes, plus header soldering

Bring: the Uno R3 and its USB data cable; Adafruit Motor Shield #1438; two TT motors #3777; a switched four-AA holder with wire leads; four matching, charged NiMH AA cells; their specified external NiMH charger; a small screwdriver; wire stripper; multimeter; and masking tape. Keep the battery switch off. The printer can rest for this session.

Finish line: you can point to every connection, measure the pack, and identify the switch that removes motor power. No motor movement is required yet.

02A · Fit the shield once

A shield is a circuit board that plugs into the Uno's rows of sockets. The #1438 product is currently called v3; the guide and Arduino library retain the V2 name. This path uses that product with an Uno R3. An old v1 shield or an L293D lookalike needs different instructions.

  1. Unplug USB and remove the AA cells. Look at the shield's underside. It needs soldered header pins to connect to the Uno; pins loosely resting in holes do not make a working circuit.
  2. If the headers are loose, complete Adafruit's standard-header assembly sequence now. Use the included straight headers. Current kits normally have the screw terminals fitted already, so inspect your actual board before repeating those older steps. Keep both boards unpowered.
  3. For a first soldering session, practice a few joints on scrap perfboard before the shield. Use an iron stand, eye protection, and ventilation. Heat the pad and pin together, feed solder into the joint, then remove solder and iron. Inspect for a joined pad and pin with no bridges to adjacent pads. If a joint is unclear, ask a makerspace helper to inspect it before powering the board. The Adafruit soldering guide supplies close-up examples to compare against.
  4. Align the shield's labeled pin rows with the Uno. Press it in evenly; no row should hang one position off the end. Check that the shield underside cannot touch the metal USB connector. Add a small piece of electrical insulating tape over the metal USB shell if the shield could touch it.
  5. Find the small removable VIN/power jumper near the motor-power terminals. Pull the plastic jumper cap off and put it in your parts box. Leave it off for this entire rover build.

The Uno will receive USB power from your computer. The motors will receive power from their own pack. The shield connects their signal ground references through its headers. You do not add another ground jumper or join their positive supply rails. This follows Adafruit's USB plus separate motor supply arrangement.

Use Collin’s Lab: Soldering as a visual companion to the header instructions. Watch where the iron touches both the pad and pin, then compare the finished joint with your practice board. Stop the video and inspect one joint before moving on.

02B · Check the pack, then connect it

  1. Fit four matching, freshly charged NiMH AA cells in the switched holder, following its polarity marks. This is a nominal 4.8 V pack. Charge the cells only in the charger specified for them; neither the Uno nor this shield charges them.
  2. Put the multimeter's black lead in COM and red lead in the V socket. Select DC volts, using a 20 V range if your meter has manual ranges. Switch the holder on. Touch black to its black lead and red to its red lead without letting the bare ends meet. Expect a positive voltage between 4.5 and 6.0 V. If it is outside that range, stop and check cell count, chemistry, charge and polarity before connecting it. Switch off again.
  3. With USB still disconnected, connect the holder's red lead to the shield's motor-power + terminal and black lead to its motor-power −/GND terminal. Use the two-terminal power block, not a motor output and not an Uno pin. Tighten onto bare conductor, leaving no loose strands outside. Give each wire a gentle tug.
  4. Leave the motors disconnected for this check. Switch the pack on: the shield's motor-power indicator should light. Switch it off again. A dark indicator means stop here and check the pack switch, cell orientation and terminal polarity.

The meter stays in voltage mode. A current-meter connection is different and can short a supply if placed across it. This session needs only voltage measurements. Keep the leads in COM and V.

02C · Give the left and right motors a home

Place the chassis with its intended front pointing away from you. Label its left motor L and right motor R. These names mean the robot's left and right, not whichever side you happen to be standing on.

Do not connect either motor wire to the Uno's 5 V pin. Tape the loose wires out of the shafts' way and keep the motor-power switch reachable.

The three electrical ideas you need today

Voltage is a compatibility requirement. Our TT motors are specified for 3–6 V, while this driver needs at least 4.5 V. Four charged NiMH cells give us an overlapping operating range; they do not stay there forever. Once the rover can run on its stand, we will check that the pack remains at or above 4.5 V while the motors turn.

Current rises when a motor works harder. The TT motor page supports this driver family but warns that stalled motors can draw high current. The shield is rated at 1.2 A per motor bridge, with a much shorter peak allowance. This combination is for a light rover rolling freely. A trapped wheel is a reason to switch off, not a test of strength. A low PWM setting does not guarantee a safe stall current.

The driver does the electrical lifting. The Uno sends small control signals. The shield switches power from the pack into the motors. Keeping these jobs separate helps the program keep running when a motor starts.

Bench note · what “4.8 V” and “1.2 A” actually tell you

The pack's nominal voltage is a convenient label; the measured voltage changes with charge and load. A driver's current rating is a limit under stated thermal conditions, not a current it forces through the motor. Do not substitute a high-current battery and assume the driver has become stronger. Electrical input power is voltage times current: 5 V at 0.3 A is 1.5 W. Some becomes motion; some becomes heat.

A meter may miss a very brief voltage dip. If the Uno repeatedly resets when motors start, disconnect motor power and recheck the removed jumper, solder joints, USB connection and pack condition before changing code.

Done for today

The jumper is off, the pack's measured voltage is written in your notebook, M1 is left, M2 is right, and the pack switch is off. Take one clear photograph of the wiring. Leave that photo beside your saved CAD file.
