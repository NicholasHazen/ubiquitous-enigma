Chapter 01 · Plastic becomes a robot part

Print a home
for two motors.

A practical printing path for your Bambu Lab X1 Carbon: start with a fit strip and a rover deck, then learn to design joints and mounts that fit on purpose.

A robot chassis is a place to hold things in the right relationship. We’ll give two motors a flat deck, let a ball caster support the front, and leave the top open so you can reach every wire. Cable ties make the first revision forgiving. You can cut one and move a motor without throwing away the whole print.

The printable starter parts below are included in this HTML file. They are dimensioned design examples, checked as closed meshes; they have not been physically printed or fitted to your parts. That is why our first task is small enough to hold between two fingers.

Start here · Give your X1 Carbon a repeatable baseline

10 minutes · Bambu Studio, plain PLA, your installed build plate · Win: a saved starting recipe

Your X1 Carbon can handle this rover. The useful new skill is deciding which surfaces must fit, then checking those surfaces. Automatic calibration helps the printing process; it does not certify that your shaft fits your hole.

  1. Select Bambu Lab X1 Carbon and the nozzle actually fitted. This guide assumes the standard 0.4 mm nozzle. Select the plate type printed on the plate you are using. Keep the plate-specific temperature and surface-preparation instructions; there is no single bed-temperature recipe for every X1 plate.
  2. Use one plain, opaque PLA spool you already print successfully. Select its matching filament preset, or Generic PLA for an ordinary third-party PLA. Keep that spool for the fit experiments. Leave the filament’s existing flow ratio and temperature settings alone for the first test.
  3. Start from the 0.20 mm Standard process for that printer/nozzle. Set three wall loops, four top/bottom shell layers, and 20% infill. Keep supports off for our flat parts and the normal print-speed mode. Save a copy as X1C-PLA-robot-baseline.
  4. For a readable baseline, set manual X-Y hole compensation and X-Y contour compensation to 0 mm; leave Auto circle contour-hole compensation off if your Studio version offers it. Keep and record the preset’s elephant-foot setting. These settings affect different things; we will diagnose before changing them.
  5. Follow Studio’s PLA chamber/door advice for the selected filament and plate. If it warns about heat buildup, vent the front door and/or top cover as directed before starting. Keep bed leveling enabled and follow any calibration compatibility prompts rather than forcing an unsupported plate/material combination.

Ready means: the preview names your actual nozzle, plate, and PLA; the saved project records the settings. Keep a native Fusion design for editable geometry and a Bambu Studio 3MF project for this print arrangement. An STL alone does not remember your slicing recipe.

Bench 01A · Print a tiny question

15–25 minutes active work + printing · PLA, slicer, one M3 screw · Win: know which slot fits

“M3” means a metric screw with a nominal 3 mm thread diameter. A 3 mm gap in CAD may print smaller than 3 mm. Our strip has four open slots: 2.8, 3.0, 3.2, and 3.4 mm, increasing from left to right when the slots face away from you. We are testing slot clearance, not certifying the size of round holes.

Download the clearance strip · STL

  1. Save clearance-strip.stl in your project folder and open it in your slicer. Set units to millimetres if asked. It should measure 40 × 20 × 4 mm. If it is much larger or smaller, fix the units before slicing.
  2. Place the broad 40 × 20 mm face on the bed. Use the X1C baseline you just saved. Preview the first layer, the slots, and the top: all four openings must remain visible. Print it.
  3. Let it cool, remove it, and label the slots 2.8, 3.0, 3.2, 3.4 with a fine pen. Hold the threaded part of your M3 screw beside each slot and slide it in gently. Do not screw it into the plastic.
  4. Record the smallest slot that accepts the screw without force. Try your 2.5 mm cable tie in the 3.4 mm slot too.

You should see: at least one slot accepts the screw, and the cable tie passes through the widest slot. The narrow slots may be deliberately too tight.

If all four are tight: check for a flared first layer. Try the screw from the upper face. If that works, the bottom edge is the problem: use the diagnosis table to check elephant-foot compensation and reprint this tiny strip. If both faces are tight, measure the outside width against 40 mm and follow that same table before printing the deck. Don’t force the fastener and call it a fit.

Make the measurement useful

Clean the caliper jaws, close them gently, and zero them. Measure the strip’s 40 mm outside span three times, lifting and replacing the jaws each time. Keep them square and use light pressure. Compare near the upper face and near the bed face; a wider bottom suggests a local first-layer bulge. A display that shows hundredths of a millimetre does not guarantee that accuracy.

Use the actual screw as the fit test; tiny holes and thread peaks are awkward caliper targets. Record the result as “slides freely,” “needs force,” or “too loose for this job.” Clearance is intentional space. Tolerance is the acceptable variation in a dimension. “This motor must not shift in its mount” is the requirement that makes those numbers useful.

Download the fit log CSV or copy its columns into your notebook. Three readings of one object check your measuring consistency. Before committing to a critical fit, print three specimens with the same saved settings and compare their ranges; that also samples print-to-print variation.

Prediction: if you double the gap, does the printed wall get stronger? No—the gap changes clearance, and it also removes material. Fit and strength are related design constraints, but they are different questions.

Next: keep the successful strip beside the printer and make the deck in Bench 01B.

Bench 01B · Give the wheels a home

20 minutes active work + printing · Same PLA profile · Win: a flat deck and adjustable caster height

The deck is 120 mm wide, 160 mm long, and 4 mm thick. Its rear is narrowed to 90 mm to clear the wheels. Four narrow slots hold the motors underneath; each tie returns around the outer edge of the deck. Four central slots help strap down the battery. The two short slots near the front are for the caster. The spacer pieces go between caster and deck so you can bring all three contacts onto the floor.

Download the rover deck · STL

Download each spacer. Your slicer can duplicate the parts:

  • 8 mm spacer — print three.
  • 4 mm spacer — print one.
  • 2 mm spacer — print two.
  • 1 mm spacer — print two.
  1. Slice the deck flat with the same successful PLA settings. Confirm its 120 × 160 mm footprint fits the printable area without clipping. Inspect the layer preview: all slots must stay open. Print and cool it.
  2. Print the spacer set with its broad faces flat. Each piece is 24 × 24 mm with two slots. Keep the thin spacers together in a labelled bag.
  3. Put the unpowered motors underneath, near the narrow rear corners as drawn. Their metal motor cans point toward the front; their output shafts point outwards. Put both axle centres on a line 35 mm forward of the rear edge. Each motor body centre is about 40 mm to its side of the deck centreline. Run each cable tie through an inner slot, across the top to the nearby outside edge, then around the motor body and back to the tie head. Use two ties and a thin foam pad per motor. Keep straps away from shafts, wires, and motor terminals; slide a strap along its slot to clear protrusions. Keep ties loose for now.
  4. Fit the rubber tyre to each wheel, then press the wheel onto its matching motor shaft and use its supplied screw. Support the gearbox while fitting it. Slide the motors until the wheels clear the deck by at least 2 mm, then tighten the ties enough to stop movement without crushing the motor housing. Check the tyre-to-deck gap visually; turn a wheel gently only if its gearbox permits it. Do not force a stiff gearbox. Chapter 04 checks free rotation under motor power.
  5. Put the rover on a flat surface. Hold the deck level. Measure the gap from the front underside to the floor; subtract the caster’s 16 mm height. Build that thickness from the spacers, to the nearest millimetre. For a measured 42 mm gap, use 26 mm of spacers: 8 + 8 + 8 + 2.
  6. Fit the caster beneath that stack using two M3 bolts inserted from the caster side up through the spacers and deck, with washers and nuts above the deck. Start with the 45 mm bolts. Check that enough thread emerges to engage each nut fully and that bolt heads cannot touch the ball. Spare thread points upward. Use a shorter bolt from the kit if necessary. Tighten gently; thin spacers should sit flat.

You should see: both tyres and the caster touch the surface; the deck is approximately level; wheels turn without rubbing; neither motor slides when you lightly push its body. Nothing is powered.

If the motor rocks: move the foam under a broad part of its plastic case and retighten both ties. Keep straps away from the axle and exposed metal motor terminals. If the caster hangs in the air: add spacer thickness. If it lifts a drive wheel: remove a little. Do this fit check before adding electronics.

Bench 01C · Make the tiny strip yours in Fusion

20–30 minutes · Fusion · Win: change one dimension deliberately

You have a printable part to keep the build moving. Now we’ll remake the small strip in Fusion so you own one piece of the design. A sketch is its flat outline. Extrude gives that outline thickness.

  1. Create a new design and set document units to millimetres. Create a component named clearance_strip. Start a sketch on the XY plane.
  2. Draw a two-point rectangle with one corner at the origin. Use the sketch dimension tool to set it to 40 mm along X and 20 mm along Y. Finish the sketch, then extrude the rectangle 4 mm.
  3. Start a sketch on the top face. Draw four rectangular notches extending 6 mm inward from the back edge. Place their centres at X = 5, 15, 25, and 35 mm from the left edge. Dimension their widths to 2.8, 3.0, 3.2, and 3.4 mm.
  4. Finish the sketch. Select the four notch profiles and extrude them through the strip with operation Cut. Save as clearance-strip-v1.
  5. Edit the widest slot dimension from 3.4 to 3.6 mm. Confirm only that slot changes. Save a new version and export a mesh in millimetres. You do not need another print today unless your first strip failed the fit test.

You should see: a 40 × 20 × 4 mm strip with four slots, and a history you can edit. You changed fit without redrawing the whole part.

If Extrude fills a notch instead of removing it: check that you selected the notch’s closed profile and that the operation is Cut. If a profile will not select, zoom into its corners and close the sketch gaps. Ask AI to explain that one failed step using a screenshot; keep the part small.

Name the dimension you expect to change

Open Modify → Change Parameters. Add slot_width = 3.4 mm and edit the widest notch’s dimension to use slot_width. Change the parameter to 3.6 mm and watch only that notch change. That is your first reusable fit decision. Autodesk’s video on parameters and constrained sketches shows the workflow: stop when you can drive one sketch dimension by name, then repeat it on your strip.

For a future pocket around a measured 20.0 mm-wide part, use part_width = 20 mm, clearance_each_side = 0.2 mm, and pocket_width = part_width + 2 * clearance_each_side. The resulting 20.4 mm pocket leaves 0.2 mm on each side in the design. Print a short slice of that pocket before making a full mount. The 0.2 mm is a trial value, not an X1 Carbon guarantee.

When exporting a round feature from Fusion, use a sufficiently fine mesh and inspect its outline in Studio. A visibly faceted hole is a geometry/export problem. Save the source design and verify millimetres and overall dimensions after import; do not resize the whole robot to repair one tight hole.

A visual refresher if Fusion feels rusty

Open Autodesk’s Introduction to 3D modeling video series. Use the sketch and extrude lessons to answer one question: how does a flat profile become a solid or a cut? Then return to the strip. You can leave the rest of the series for another build.

Optional · Why a thicker print does not solve every problem

The floor pushes on the tyre, the tyre loads the shaft, and the motor housing transfers that load into the straps and deck. That route is the load path. A loose strap fails its job even if the deck is very thick. A narrow upright tab can fail across layer bonds even when the rest of a print feels solid.

PLA is our first indoor prototype material because you already know how to print it. Heat and sustained load can deform it. Later, choosing another material or print orientation is a response to an observed problem, not a requirement to finish this rover. Precision 1 · Find a fit with a small hole-and-pin test

25 minutes active work + printing · Calipers, one M3 screw, the saved PLA baseline · Win: a documented clearance that works for this pair

A nice-looking deck does not tell you whether a round joint will fit. Print this smaller question before an arm bracket. The coupon is 60 × 30 × 4 mm. Hold its clipped corner at the upper right: the nearby row is 3.0, 3.2, 3.4, 3.6, 3.8 mm; the other row is 6.0, 6.2, 6.4, 6.6 mm, both left to right. Those are nominal CAD diameters.

Download the round-fit coupon · Download its 6 mm test pin

  1. Print the coupon flat and the pin standing on its 12 mm-wide base, using the unchanged baseline. All hole axes point up. The pin has a 10 mm-long nominal 6 mm shaft above a 2 mm base. Let both parts cool before testing.
  2. Label the holes. Try the actual M3 screw in the small row from both faces, without turning or forcing it. Record the smallest hole that lets it pass. These are clearance holes, not printed screw threads.
  3. Measure the pin’s straight shaft at two heights and two directions, avoiding the base junction. Record the regular shaft size and inspect any seam bump separately: that high spot can decide whether the whole pin passes. Try it in the large row. Find the smallest hole through which it slides with light finger pressure; record any catch or wobble. Do not press it into a tight hole.
  4. Repeat the successful candidate before committing to a complete joint. If none passes, use the diagnostic section below. Keep the same material, orientation, and settings when transferring this result to another part.

The useful arithmetic: diameter clearance is hole diameter − pin diameter. A measured 6.25 mm hole around a measured 6.05 mm pin has 0.20 mm diametral clearance, or 0.10 mm radial clearance per side when centered. These are illustrative measurements. Your printed dimensions decide the fit; the 6.4 mm label alone does not.

Done: a labelled pair slides as intended, and its material/profile/orientation are in your log. This pin teaches mating geometry; use purchased shafts or bearings when a real mechanism needs low wear or controlled play.

Precision 2 · Make the fit survive the load

20 minutes · Your next bracket in Fusion and Studio · Win: explain its orientation and load path

Pick the servo base from Chapter 07 as your next design. Trace a push from the horn into the case, the straps, and the base. That route is the load path. Then rotate the part in Studio and inspect the layer preview: which thin section would be pulled apart across layers, and which mating surface would be left rough by supports?

  1. Start with the broad base flat and the fit surfaces clear of supports. For an upright bracket, compare a side-down orientation or a two-piece bolted design before accepting a critical supported hole.
  2. Keep the initial three walls. If a mounting region needs more material, add a broad rib or thicken that local section in CAD; preview that material around the actual screw hole. Infill percentage alone does not repair a narrow tab or poor layer direction.
  3. Keep a hole axis vertical where the mechanism permits it. If it must print sideways, make a separate coupon in that exact orientation. A sagging horizontal bore is not the same error as a small vertical hole.
  4. Print one part, assemble it unpowered, and apply a gentle representative load. Look for movement at straps, fasteners, and thin sections. Record the first place that flexes. This screens a prototype; it does not establish a safe rated load.

Read the orientation, support, and tolerance sections of Prusa’s design guide. Its geometry lessons transfer to your X1C; its printer-specific performance numbers do not. Return when you can point to one surface you would keep away from support material.

Precision 3 · Build joints you can service

20 minutes · Hardware and one small mount · Win: fasten without forcing the fit

Keep the first robot on through-bolts, washers, and nuts. The bolt clears the printed hole; the nut supplies the thread. Leave room for the screwdriver and a way to hold the nut before placing holes in Fusion. Tighten until the parts stay located, without crushing the plastic. Recheck after use: printed plastic can relax under sustained clamp load.

For a captive-nut pocket, measure the actual nut across its flats and its thickness. Add a named clearance to the pocket and print only that corner first. A lead-in helps insertion; enough surrounding material prevents the pocket from splitting. For a pivot, arrange a purchased smooth shaft, shoulder screw, or bearing so tightening the fastener does not clamp the moving member solid.

Next upgrade · Heat-set inserts, one coupon first

Use the hole diameter, depth, wall thickness, and installation guidance for the exact insert you buy. “M3” names its internal thread; it does not specify the outside knurl or its required printed hole. Make a separate test boss, install one insert with an appropriate temperature-controlled tool, let it cool, and check that a screw enters squarely without the insert turning.

Watch CNC Kitchen’s insert installation video and guide for seating and alignment. Then read the author’s hole-size experiment with one question: why does an actual printed bore matter more than a copied CAD diameter? His test setup is evidence for testing your fit, not a load rating for your arm. Inserts are not required for the first rover or pointer.

Done: you can assemble and remove the joint, reach its fasteners, and distinguish clearance, clamping, and rotation.

Repair loop · Change the cause, then reprint the small test

Keep your original 3MF and change one thing in a copy. First confirm the print is complete, flat, and cooled, with the correct nozzle, plate, material, and dimensions. Use the symptom to choose the next action.

You observeDo this next
Only the bottom lip catchesCompare top and bottom. In a copied process, increase the recorded elephant-foot compensation by 0.05 mm—for example, 0.15 to 0.20 mm. Preview and reprint the strip. Restore the prior value if the first layer loses needed material or adhesion. Leave full-height dimensions alone.
An open slot or pocket is tight; the outside span is closeUse Bench 01C to increase that feature’s CAD clearance, then reprint the small strip. An open notch is part of the outside contour, not an enclosed hole; do not use hole compensation to repair it.
A vertical hole is tight throughout; outside size is closeFor one designed fit, increase its CAD clearance and reprint that feature. If several holes show a repeatable similar error, try +0.05 mm manual X-Y hole compensation on the coupon, with automatic circle compensation off. That adds about 0.10 mm to an ideal sliced hole diameter. Remeasure before keeping it.
Outside dimensions are consistently too large, but extrusion looks soundCheck more than one span and specimen. Try −0.05 mm manual X-Y contour compensation in a copied process. It reduces outside boundaries; keep hole settings unchanged and remeasure both. Restore the original if another required feature becomes too small.
Rough tops, gaps, or excess material occur broadlyCheck nozzle/filament preset and extrusion health. Use Bambu Studio’s Flow Rate calibration procedure for that filament if needed; repeat the original coupon afterward.
Bulges or gaps concentrate around speed changes and cornersCheck Flow Dynamics calibration for the actual filament and hotend, then repeat. Do not diagnose every corner defect as pressure advance without checking the print.
A sideways hole is flattened at its top, or a corner liftsFix orientation, unsupported geometry, or bed adhesion first. XY hole compensation cannot restore a collapsed roof or a warped plate.

Two calibrations, two jobs: Flow Rate adjusts the extrusion amount; Flow Dynamics compensates for pressure response as extrusion speed changes. Neither replaces a dimensional measurement. Use the linked Flow Rate procedure or Flow Dynamics procedure only when that branch applies. Bambu Lab’s short X1 linear-advance demonstration provides the visual idea; current Studio controls may differ from that early video.

Mind the offset: positive manual X-Y hole compensation enlarges enclosed holes in the XY slice; positive contour compensation enlarges outside boundaries. A +0.10 mm hole-boundary offset adds about 0.20 mm to an ideal round hole’s sliced diameter. It is not a promise of that exact change in your cooled print, and an open notch is not an enclosed hole. Read the Bambu compensation diagrams, then inspect your own preview before printing. Keep automatic circle compensation off during this manual experiment.

If extrusion changes unpredictably, inspect the spool and hotend condition before creating a permanent size correction. Dry filament according to its maker’s instructions when moisture is a plausible cause; it is not the default cure for a tight hole. Changing material, nozzle, orientation, or key process settings means checking the fit again.

Done: the changed test passes, its result repeats, and you can name the one correction that helped. Save that local recipe rather than declaring a universal “X1C tolerance.”
