The first rover gives you a way to collect observations deliberately. Its next value comes from replaying those observations: you can improve a reconstruction, compare two models, or inspect a failed prediction while the chassis is switched off. Your Windows machine and RTX 4090 provide an experimental workspace; the Spark is another possible compute host once the selected software's ARM64 and accelerator support have been checked.

The default next branch is reconstruct a small, static patch of the room from recorded RGB-D data. It builds directly on calibration, timestamps, poses, and measurement checks. The other branches below stay available after that checkpoint. These are research plans with entry and exit conditions; they are not claims that the first build already supports them.

Voxel carving is one way to update space along measured rays. The endpoint and the path leading to it contain different evidence; areas behind an occluder may remain unseen. OctoMap is a primary reference for probabilistic 3D occupancy with free, occupied, and unknown regions. Read its overview and sketch one ray hitting a chair. Research session 10A · Reconstruct one corner

Several 45–60 minute sessions · Prerequisites: replayable RGB-D, calibration, valid camera poses · Finish: inspect a measured surface and its gaps

A truncated signed distance function, or TSDF, combines depth measurements around estimated surfaces. Repeated observations can reduce noise, while wrong poses can create duplicated walls. We will use that behavior as a diagnostic. Start with a static chair and wall corner; a child or pet crossing the scan creates another problem before we understand the first one.

  1. Save a copy of one short recording and its calibration. Verify depth units and the transform direction expected by the reconstruction tool. Keep the original unchanged.
  2. Follow Open3D's TSDF integration example on its documented example data in a separate environment. Its source documentation describes integration from depth images and known poses. Record the version that actually runs.
  3. Write a small converter for your own RGB-D frames, intrinsics, and poses. Inspect three transformed frames together before integrating the complete sequence. This converter depends on the selected camera and recording format and is not supplied by the Fieldbook.
  4. Choose a coarse voxel size for the first view, then compare one finer setting on the same input. Record runtime, memory use, gaps, and changes to a few measured spans. Smaller voxels do not repair bad calibration.
  5. Extract and save the surface. Compare it with physical measurements and inspect unseen areas. Keep the navigation map unchanged while evaluating this reconstruction.

Done: the saved result has a reproducible input manifest, a stated scale, several measurement comparisons, and labelled gaps. A good-looking screenshot alone does not complete this experiment.

If surfaces double: inspect pose alignment and timing before tuning voxel resolution. If every dimension has the same scale error, inspect depth units and calibration. If shiny or dark regions are missing, compare raw depth there; smoothing cannot recover a measurement the sensor never made.

Then ask what the room looks like

Kerbl and colleagues' Gaussian-splatting work represents a scene with optimized 3D Gaussians and renders them from camera viewpoints. Watch the authors' project video, then identify its objective: reproducing appearance from images. The comparison to your geometric reconstruction is the interesting experiment.

For an implementation route, use Nerfstudio's Splatfacto guide in an isolated environment. Its custom-data workflow needs camera poses and compatible data formatting; recordings do not become a training dataset merely by being saved in a folder. The custom-data guide describes processing images and video, including a COLMAP route. Inspect overlapping, sharp views and camera alignment before fitting a scene.

Your experiment is to reconstruct the same static corner and compare appearance from held-out viewpoints. Look for blurred objects, floaters, and failures away from the capture path. Keep the geometric map as a separate artifact. Then ask what belongs in the room

ConceptGraphs demonstrates a research route from posed RGB-D images through segmentation, feature extraction, multi-view association, and a 3D object graph. Use its paper and explainer to trace those stages. Its results motivate an experiment; they do not establish accuracy in your house.

Start your own extension with two distinctive objects and a handful of viewpoints. Measure whether observations associate with the correct object, including when one moves or is briefly hidden. Keep “same category” separate from “same physical object.” Then evaluate a few contextual queries against the stored evidence. Add language interfaces only after you can inspect where their answers came from.

Test usefulness before expanding the platform

The commercial hypothesis is that a moving observer can provide useful household information that people would revisit. A first product experiment can test this with the one-room prototype: create a short report showing room layout and evidence-backed sightings of a chosen item. Ask a few prospective users to use the report for a real task, then observe whether they return to it without prompting.

Record time saved or lost, incorrect conclusions, manual corrections, setup effort, and what they actually do next. Compare the workflow with taking a phone photo or measuring the room manually. Interest in a demonstration is different evidence from repeated use. A small team also needs to understand assembly, calibration, support, and maintenance work; the prototype's run log begins to expose those costs.

Make the next investment answer one observed problem: a better sensor for missing geometry, better data for a recurring recognition error, or a clearer interface for a useful query. Pricing and demand remain unvalidated until tested. There is no need to assign a business model to every experiment.

Keep these later projects visible

Transport: a basket carrier needs a new payload, stability, traction, clearance, and stopping assessment. Surveying with a light rover does not qualify its chassis to carry laundry. Start with a measured test load along one controlled route.

Play and exercise: begin with a stationary or slow, turn-based game in a bounded area. Test whether the interaction remains fun after its novelty fades before adding pursuit or faster motion.

Arms and unusual locomotion: the previous edition's joint workshop, wiggle mechanism, and simulation experiments remain archived extensions. Their parts and assumptions belong to those builds. A later learned gait still needs a measured physical baseline, suitable sensing, a simulation model, and a transfer evaluation.
