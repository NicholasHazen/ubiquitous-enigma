01 · Give the robot a place and a question

A room to measure.
A toy to recognize.

We are building a rover that can survey a room and remember what it saw. Your first contribution is a small reference kit: a measured room sketch, labelled photographs, and a real part to test against a print.

Start the guided first session and record your result →

Picture the later result: you ask what the rover noticed near a marked place in the room, and it can show you an image, a capture time, and the position it was observing from. That starts with two things we can check ourselves: where the room's surfaces are, and whether one specific object is visible in a photograph. Today you make those reference observations before asking a robot or model to reproduce them.

There are three working sessions below. Do one at a time; together they may take more than an hour. Session 01A produces notes/room-reference.md. Session 01B produces twenty image files and notes/capture-A.md. Session 01C produces a measured row in notes/fit-log.csv. You do not need a built robot or new hardware purchases to start the room and image work.

The first model answers a question about the whole image: “Is this particular toy visible?” We will later connect that prediction to the rover's capture pose. A positive answer will not locate the toy within the image, prove the floor is safe to drive across, or establish that the toy is still there now.

Session 01A · Define the room your rover will learn

20–35 minutes of active work · Phone or camera, tape measure, paper and a text editor · Result: one room-reference note with three measured spans and a target-object rule

Your objective: make a reference that another person could use to identify your test area, repeat your three measurements, and label a photograph of your chosen toy.

Those measurements will later give us an independent way to check the robot's map.

  1. Choose the first test area and the object. Use one room, or a clearly bounded part of a room, with three accessible distances you can measure. A clear patch of hardwood is a useful starting place. Pick one distinctive, movable toy: for example, a particular brightly coloured block or small figure. Record: the room/boundary and “The first learned observation is whether [this specific object] is visibly present.” Keep this rule fixed through the first model comparison. An unclear or heavily hidden object is a reason to retake an image, not guess its label.
  2. Create the local place for this work. In File Explorer, create home-rover somewhere you can find again, then create notes, cad, data, and runs inside it. All paths in the course are relative to that folder. This is your robot project's working folder, separate from the textbook's GitHub repository. Create notes/room-reference.md in your text editor and copy the blank template below. In Windows Notepad, use Save as type → All files so the name does not become room-reference.md.txt. Record: your actual folder path and the two decisions from step 1. Keep household images and large recordings local by default; if you use Git, exclude those files rather than committing the entire working folder.
  3. Make the room recognizable on paper. Photograph the test area. Draw a top-down outline on paper or in a drawing tool; a rough sketch is enough. Mark fixed features such as wall faces, a door jamb, or a cabinet face. Mark the chosen test boundary if you are using only part of the room. Save a photograph or image of the sketch beside the room photograph in notes/. Record: both file paths. For eventual powered trials, the rover stays on this floor: stair access will be physically blocked and the test area will be clear of children and pets.
  4. Measure three spans you can repeat. Label them R1, R2, and R3 on your sketch. Choose one longer span and two shorter spans, with at least two directions represented. For each span, mark its start and end surfaces, hold the tape along a straight line, and read the distance. Keep the measuring height consistent for that span; do not silently swap a wall face for a protruding skirting board. Record the height or other detail another person would need to place the tape the same way. Record: the two endpoints, the measured length in metres, the tool, and the method or limitation for every span. Recheck any reading whose endpoint or tape alignment is uncertain.
  5. Check your reference against the room. Open the saved note and sketch, then point to the actual start and end of each span. Check that the object rule identifies one particular toy. Record: the date and any unresolved limitation, such as a cabinet preventing a wall-to-wall measurement. Keep the true boundary of what you measured: a distance across part of the room is not automatically the room's full width.

Save as notes/room-reference.md. Replace every bracket with your own decision, path, or observation. The blank lengths are places for your measurements.

Check the result: your note identifies the test boundary and target rule, links the photo/sketch, and records three measured spans with endpoints, units, and method. Someone using it can point to the same endpoints in the room. Keep uncertain readings marked as uncertain.

You now have something the future map can be compared against. The next session reuses the same toy and label rule to begin your model's training data.

Session 01B · Collect the first twenty examples

25–40 minutes of active work · The chosen toy and an existing camera · Result: ten present images, ten absent images, and one capture note

Your objective: save ten photographs with the chosen toy visibly present, ten with it absent, and a note identifying the capture conditions.

The label should follow the toy's presence. If every present image has one background and every absent image has another, a model could learn the backgrounds and appear to recognize the toy. Varying the scene within both labels helps us test what it learns. This is the first part of the dataset your own model will learn from while you build the rover.

A capture session is a related collection taken under one set of conditions. Neighbouring video frames often carry almost the same information. Later, we will keep whole sessions together when splitting data so that we can test whether the model handles views it has not already encountered.

  1. Prepare the two labels. Open your room-reference note and keep the target rule beside you. Inside home-rover/data, create train/A/present and train/A/absent. These folders hold the photographs; the capture note belongs in notes/capture-A.md. Record: session A, the date, and the camera you are using. A phone is sufficient.
  2. Make one present/absent pair. Put the toy where a person can clearly recognize it and take a photo. Remove that toy entirely and take another photo. Save the first in present and the second in absent. Use unique names such as A_001.jpg and A_002.jpg. Check now: open both saved files and apply your written rule; this catches an export or folder mistake before you take eighteen more pictures.
  3. Build ten varied pairs. Alternate between present and absent until each folder contains ten distinct images. Across the pairs, change viewpoint, camera distance, the toy's position in the frame, and background arrangement. Move unrelated objects around in both classes. Keep the target recognizable in positive examples. Record: the kinds of variation you introduced and the lighting/background conditions. Twenty near-identical burst frames are not the intended result.
  4. Inspect the files you will actually train with. Export ordinary JPEG or PNG files. Remove location metadata if you intend to share them, and avoid including people, documents, or screens unnecessarily. Open all twenty files. Correct a wrong folder, and recapture an ambiguous or unreadable image instead of guessing its label. Record: the readable count in each folder and any correction you made.
  5. Save the capture conditions and the boundary of this result. Complete the note below. Keep all images beneath the same home-rover/data directory. Record: one condition not covered yet—for example, another lighting setup or a different background. If you only managed a simpler set today, describe that accurately; do not duplicate files to fill the count.

Save as notes/capture-A.md. This note preserves the conditions and counts; you do not need to create the training manifest yet.

A manifest is a table that tells the training program where each image lives, which capture session it belongs to, and which label it has. The complete experiment will collect sessions A–H: A–D for training, E–F for validation while choosing the model, and G–H for the reserved final test. Every session contains both labels. Twenty images per session is an initial collection target, not a promise that 160 images will solve household recognition. Today you only need session A.

Check the result: ten distinct present files and ten distinct absent files open correctly, agree with your label rule, and are in the matching session folders. The capture note records their conditions and counts. Session A is useful saved data; the complete training program still needs the later validation and test sessions.

The model track now has its first real input. Next, we will give the mechanical track an equally concrete reference: the part a printed opening must fit.

Session 01C · Measure the part your first print must fit

15–20 minutes of active work · Calipers and a real M3 bolt or smooth pin · Result: one measured mating-part row in the fit log

Your objective: save repeatable measurements of a physical part you will keep beside the printer.

A label such as “M3” identifies a hardware family; it does not tell us which opening in your printed coupon will accept that particular bolt.

  1. Choose and label one part. Use an M3 bolt if you have one; it will test the clearance strip in Chapter 02. A smooth pin is also useful, provided you record what it is and choose a matching coupon later. Keep the part in a labelled bag or small container. Record: a specimen ID such as bolt-A, the feature to measure, and the nominal size only if it is known.
  2. Check the measuring tool. Clean the outside caliper jaws, close them gently, and check zero. Set millimetres. Measure across the bolt's thread crests or the smooth pin's outside diameter with light, consistent contact. Avoid measuring a bolt head when you intend to fit its threaded body. Record: the tool and zero-check result.
  3. Take three readings. Measure the diameter, release the jaws, rotate or reposition the part, and measure again. Repeat once more. Keep the jaws square to the feature. Record: all three readings in millimetres and where/direction they were taken; do not replace them with the nominal size. If the readings differ noticeably, recheck zero and contact before deciding that the part or printer is inaccurate.
  4. Save a row for the actual mating part. Download the fit-log template and save it as notes/fit-log.csv. Open it in a spreadsheet or text editor. Add a new row for your labelled part. Use specimen_id and feature to identify it; put the three readings in actual_1_mm, actual_2_mm, and actual_3_mm. Put tool, zero check, and contact directions in notes. The supplied pin-A and coupon rows are for later printed specimens; do not relabel them as a bolt you measured today. Leave print settings and printed-fit results blank until those checks happen.
  5. Name the next print. Open the X1 Carbon recipe session. With the M3 bolt, the next fit experiment uses the clearance strip; save its name in your fit-log notes and keep the same bolt for that test. If using a different pin, record the matching coupon choice and any geometry you still need to check. Record: the coupon decision and the saved fit-log path. You will choose and save the actual printer recipe in the next session, before printing.

Check the result: the saved log identifies the real part, contains three actual readings with units and method, and names the next small print. You can retrieve the same physical part. No printed clearance has been established yet.

You now have three small, useful artifacts: room measurements to check a map, photographs to teach a model, and a mating part to check a print. The next physical step is to save a repeatable X1 Carbon recipe and print the smallest uncertain fit. While that print runs, the image collection can grow in parallel.
