Chapter 02 · Hardware track

Design a chassis
that fits.

A pair of wheels needs to hold its alignment. A scanner needs a steady view. Your first assembly gives both a place to live.

We are building a small survey rover for a bounded patch of hardwood floor. Two powered wheels steer it by turning at different speeds; a third contact supports the body. The first chassis carries its own components. Laundry, thresholds, and climbing loads belong to later designs. We will learn more from a removable motor bracket and a measured mounting hole than from an elaborate shell that hides the wires.

You have designed individual parts in Fusion. The next skill is preserving relationships between parts: the wheels share an axle line, the deck clears their tyres, the support keeps the body level, and each component can be removed. We will start from real dimensions and print the smallest uncertain fit before printing an entire deck.

What is supplied: the small fit coupons and measurement sheets are downloadable below. The new survey chassis is a guided custom-design exercise; a finished, physically validated CAD assembly is not supplied. The old TT-motor deck belongs to the archived first rover and does not match this drivetrain.

Choose the reference drivetrain

The default uses two Pololu #4885 47:1 low-power 12 V encoder gearmotors, a pair of #2676 metal motor brackets, and #3690 80 mm wheels with their 4 mm D-shaft collets. The bracket and wheel interfaces are purchased; your deck, equipment trays, and sensor mounts are the design work. This keeps the first precision exercise focused on accessible printed features.

A RoboClaw controller, motor battery, and small onboard computer will need room too. We will specify their electrical interfaces in Chapter 04. For now, use the component build sheet to record actual dimensions, mass, connectors, and mounting patterns. It is a staged specification, not a promise that every currently sold variation fits together. Confirm the exact part revision before ordering the matching mount.

Session · Save an X1 Carbon baseline

15 minutes setup · Plain PLA and the installed build plate · Output: a repeatable print recipe

Use the PLA you already print successfully. Select the actual X1 Carbon nozzle and plate in Bambu Studio; the starting geometry assumes a 0.4 mm nozzle. Start from 0.20 mm Standard, three walls, four top and bottom layers, 20% infill, normal speed, and supports off for the flat coupons. Save a copy as X1C-survey-PLA-baseline and save the arranged print as a 3MF project.

Keep the matching filament preset and plate preparation guidance. Record the filament, nozzle, plate, process version, and material batch in your fit log. Follow the Studio guidance for chamber ventilation with PLA and your plate. Changing material, orientation, nozzle, or process later means checking important fits again.

For the first fit experiment, set manual X-Y hole and contour compensation to zero; leave automatic circle compensation off if your version offers it. Record the existing elephant-foot compensation. These controls address different geometry errors. Bambu's compensation reference is useful when diagnosing a repeatable error; it does not supply a universal tolerance for your printer.

Done: the saved project identifies the actual printer configuration and a single material. Automatic calibration and a neat-looking print are useful process signals; neither proves a mating part will fit.

Session · Print a small question

30 minutes active work plus printing · Calipers and an M3 screw · Output: a measured fit

Download the 40 × 20 × 4 mm clearance strip. Its open slots are nominally 2.8, 3.0, 3.2, and 3.4 mm, left to right with the openings facing away from you. Print it flat with the unchanged baseline. While it prints, return to the image collection in Chapter 03.

  1. After cooling, label the slots. Slide the threaded portion of an actual M3 screw into each slot with light finger pressure. Do not cut a thread by turning it. Record the smallest comfortable fit.
  2. Zero the calipers, then repeat measurements at the top and bottom of the strip. Look for a first-layer lip. Repeat each reading rather than treating the display resolution as measurement accuracy.
  3. Print the round-hole coupon and 6 mm test pin when a round fit matters. Hold the coupon's clipped corner at upper right: its nearby row is 3.0, 3.2, 3.4, 3.6, 3.8 mm, and the other row is 6.0, 6.2, 6.4, 6.6 mm, both left to right. Print the coupon flat and the pin standing on its broad base.
  4. Measure the pin at two heights and directions; inspect the seam separately. Try the holes without forcing. Keep the first passing pair and repeat it before transferring the fit to a larger mount.

A hole 6.25 mm across around a 6.05 mm shaft leaves 0.20 mm diametral clearance. If centred, that is 0.10 mm on each side. Those numbers are an example, not measurements of the supplied STL. A supported sideways hole can behave differently from this vertical hole, so test the orientation you intend to use.

Done: you can show a fitting pair and its measured dimensions, material, and orientation. Keep that pair beside the printer. It is your evidence for this local design choice.

Default route: after the fit coupon, go to Chapter 03 and train your first models. The remaining layout, Fusion mount and assembly sessions below are your return point once component drawings and parts are available. Complete them before Chapter 04.

Session · Make the robot fit on paper

45–60 minutes active work · Fusion, ruler, component drawings · Output: an assembly layout

Start a Fusion design in millimetres. Create separate components named deck, left_drive, right_drive, support, and electronics. A component represents a replaceable thing; it is more useful here than one body containing every shape. Put the origin halfway between the wheel contact centres on the nominal floor plane. We will later use x forward, y left, and z up in the robot software. In this coordinate system the floor is z = 0; the deck needs a positive height above it.

Place the first part in three dimensions

A top view can hide a deck that passes through the floor. Establish the side view before adding detail. In Modify → Change Parameters, add the following named lengths in millimetres. The 40 mm radius below is an illustration from an unloaded 80 mm wheel; use your measured component geometry for the design.

ParameterMeaning and source
wheel_radiusAxle height above the nominal floor. Start with the wheel drawing and check the loaded assembly later.
axle_to_deck_bottomSigned vertical distance from motor-shaft centre to the deck's underside, determined by the chosen bracket orientation and drawing.
deck_bottom_zwheel_radius + axle_to_deck_bottom. This is the offset of the deck sketch plane above the floor.
deck_thickness4 mm as an initial prototype thickness; it does not establish a rated load.

For example, a hypothetical bracket placing the deck underside 15 mm above an axle at 40 mm produces deck_bottom_z = 55 mm. Those are teaching values, not dimensions of the selected Pololu bracket. Verify the real distance and sign from its drawing and delivered part.

  1. Activate the deck component. Choose Construct → Offset Plane, select the XY origin plane, and enter deck_bottom_z. Sketch a provisional 220 × 180 mm deck rectangle on that plane, then extrude upward by deck_thickness. Inspect a side view: the wheels reach the floor and the deck clears it.

After the next mounting-coupon session creates the actual attachment geometry, use the rigid-joint exercise below to preserve the bracket’s relationship to the deck.

The distinction between a sketch constraint and an assembly joint matters here: the constraint controls geometry inside a component; the joint controls the relationship between components. Use Autodesk's offset-plane instructions, joint instructions, and grounding reference only as each operation becomes relevant. The wheel can remain a static envelope during this packaging exercise.

  1. Treat the initial deck size as a packaging trial that fits within the X1C's build area, not a required final size or a rated structural design. Use simple boxes and cylinders for the purchased components; include plug and cable-bend space.
  2. Place the motor axes on the same transverse line, with wheels outside the deck. Keep the motor bodies accessible from below and leave room to remove the wheel. Measure the wheel-centre separation; name it track_width. It is not the deck width.
  3. Place the third contact ahead of the axle line and put heavy components low, between the support contacts. Viewed from above, the projected centre of mass should remain inside their support triangle. Leave a clear route to the power disconnect.
  4. Reserve a stable upper location for the lidar with an unobstructed horizontal scan. Reserve a forward camera mount and cable route. A tall, flexible mast makes every bump look like sensor motion, so begin short and stiff.
  5. Draw the actual fastener heads, washers, nuts, and tool access as clearance envelopes. Choose two bracket mounting points separated as widely as the bracket permits. Use its current drawing and your measurement; this bracket family has had different hole spacing across revisions.

Done: the layout has a shared axle line, room for cables, three support contacts, and a reachable fastener for every replaceable component. Save a screenshot with the front direction marked. If two parts collide, move their simple envelopes now.

Session · Turn the successful fit into a mount

45–60 minutes active work · Fusion and one delivered bracket · Output: a mounting coupon

Reuse deck_thickness and add named parameters for screw_clearance, pocket_clearance_per_side, and the measured bracket-hole spacing. The 4 mm deck remains a starting prototype thickness. Choose the clearance from your coupon instead of copying a number from another printer. Keep overall dimensions separate from mating clearances so a tight pocket does not tempt you to scale the whole assembly.

  1. Model only the deck region under one motor bracket: a small plate with its two selected mounting holes and enough surrounding material for washers. Position the holes from the bracket drawing, then verify against the delivered part.
  2. Constrain the sketch and use dimensions rather than dragging geometry into place. Extrude the plate; cut the holes; add a small lead-in where it helps insertion. Keep the bracket as a separate component.
  3. Export a mesh in millimetres with enough refinement that holes are visibly round in the slicer. Inspect the imported dimensions. Print the coupon in the same orientation intended for the deck.
  4. Assemble with through-bolts, washers, and nuts. The screw passes through the plastic; the nut supplies the thread. Tighten only enough to hold the bracket without crushing the plastic. Confirm that a driver can reach both screws and the bracket can be removed.
  5. Transfer the passing hole pattern into the complete deck. Add local ribs or wider mounting pads where loads enter. Inspect the layer preview around those regions; extra infill elsewhere will not repair a narrow, weak mounting tab.

Keep the fitted bracket attached in CAD

  1. Fix the deck component to the design using Pin (called Ground in older interfaces). Keep the bracket component free to move; remove an automatic Ground To Parent relationship from that component if necessary. Ground To Parent fixes a child relative to its parent, while Pin fixes its position in the design.
  2. With the deck and bracket represented by separate components, choose Assemble → Joint and Rigid. Select matching attachment locations on the bracket and deck—such as the centres of mating screw holes—and check the preview's face orientation and offset before accepting. A rigid joint removes relative motion between these bolted parts.
  3. Try dragging the bracket and inspect the side view again. It should stay attached to the pinned deck without an unexplained gap. If the bracket is already correctly positioned, an As-Built Joint → Rigid can preserve that position; it does not correct a misplaced part.

Read the orientation and assembly sections of Prusa's design guide with one question: which surface needs an accurate fit, and which direction loads it? Its geometric advice is useful across printers; machine-specific performance claims do not establish your X1C result.

Done: the bracket fits without forcing, stays located under a gentle hand load, and can be removed. Save the native Fusion design and the slicer project together.

Choose the third contact from the geometry: use a small purchased swivel caster with a bolted plate and a documented load rating above the load it will support. Measure the floor-to-mount-plane distance with the drive wheels fitted. Subtract the caster’s installed height to obtain the required spacer thickness; a negative result means that caster is too tall for this layout. Check the full swivel envelope in Fusion and test rolling resistance on hardwood. Record its exact part number and mounting drawing before printing the deck. The old rover’s caster spacers are not automatically correct for this new assembly.

Session · Assemble the rolling structure

45–60 minutes active work plus printing · Unpowered components · Output: a chassis that rolls without rubbing

Print the full deck after the local fit passes. Mount the motors using the supplied bracket-to-motor screws; excessive engagement can reach the gears. Fit the wheel's correct 4 mm D-shaft collet using the wheel maker's assembly instructions, supporting the mechanism rather than hammering the shaft. Keep wheels close enough to their supports to avoid needless overhang, with clearance for the tyre and hub to rotate.

Install the third contact using measured spacers so the deck sits level. Roll the unpowered assembly by hand over clean hardwood. Mark one point on each wheel and watch for wobble or a tyre rubbing the deck. Move the caster through its full swivel arc and check wires cannot enter a wheel. Temporarily secure representative component masses where they will actually sit, then repeat the roll check. This is a prototype inspection, not a payload rating.

Trace the load from floor to tyre, shaft, motor bracket, deck, and payload. A sensor tray that moves relative to the chassis changes the sensor's assumed position. Add bracing at the moving joint, reduce its height, or widen its support before increasing print infill. Keep ventilation openings and the physical disconnect accessible.

Done: the unpowered loaded layout sits on three contacts, rolls without visible binding, retains its parts, and has accessible fasteners. Record the overall footprint, loaded wheel radius estimate, and wheel-centre separation. We will calibrate rolling measurements after powered movement works.

If a part does not fit, take the small loop

ObservationNext action
Only the bottom edge catchesCompare top and bottom dimensions; inspect elephant foot and first-layer conditions. Change one relevant setting in a copy and reprint the coupon.
A slot or pocket is tight through its full depthIncrease that feature's CAD clearance. An open slot is not an enclosed hole, so hole compensation is the wrong control.
Several vertical holes are consistently undersizedConfirm the measurement and print quality, then compare a small manual hole-compensation trial. A boundary offset changes the diameter on both sides.
A sideways hole sags or the deck liftsChange orientation, support, or bed adhesion before applying dimensional compensation.
The deck flexes near a fastenerInspect the load path, washer support, and local rib geometry. A larger global scale changes every useful dimension.
