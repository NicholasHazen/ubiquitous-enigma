06 · Geometry you can inspect

Give the room coordinates.

A camera sees a chair. An encoder sees a wheel turn. We need a shared account of where those observations came from before they can describe your room.

Put the rover beside a wall and move it sideways by hand. The wall stayed still, but its position relative to the rover changed. That distinction is the beginning of mapping: a measurement has meaning relative to a named coordinate system, at a particular time. In this chapter, we will make those relationships visible and check them against a ruler.

We use ROS 2 Jazzy on the rover’s native Ubuntu 24.04 computer. ROS carries timestamped messages between programs; tf2 maintains the relationships between coordinate systems, called frames. The Windows workstation can display and analyze recordings. You can move suitable workloads to the Spark later, after the same recording works on the reference host.

Start here: complete the sensor inspection in Chapter 05A. This chapter expects a working sensor driver and the encoder bridge described in Chapter 04. The book does not bundle that hardware-specific bridge. If it is still being implemented, you can complete the paper geometry and depth-image exercises, then return to the bridge’s first failing acceptance check.

Session 06A · Put arrows on the robot

45–60 minutes · Tape, ruler, robot and frame sketch · Result: a measured coordinate diagram

We will use base_link as the rover’s body frame. Place its origin halfway between the drive-wheel contact points on the nominal flat floor, directly below the axle midpoint. This is a fixed point relative to the body: the axle is one effective rolling radius above it. Mark the origin’s vertical projection on the deck. Our planar wheel-odometry equations estimate the motion of this midpoint, so the published pose and the physical reference agree. Following ROS coordinate conventions, x points forward, y points left, and z points up. Distances in ROS are metres and angles are radians. Keep millimetres in your Fusion design, then convert them explicitly when entering robot geometry.

  1. Mark the origin’s projection and forward direction on the deck. Draw the left and right wheel centres, lidar centre, and camera mounting reference on one sheet.
  2. Measure each sensor’s offset from the origin. Write both the number and its unit: a mount 120 mm forward becomes x = 0.120 m. Measure sensor height from the nominal floor plane, along z. Include orientation; an offset alone cannot describe a tilted camera.
  3. Save those measurements with the CAD revision and a photograph. Moving the camera bracket later changes the calibration, even if the software stays identical.
  4. Draw this chain: map → odom → base_link → sensor frames. Label the program responsible for each connection.
FrameWhat you use it forWho supplies its relationship
base_linkA point fixed to the robot bodyThe robot description defines its fixed sensor mounts.
odomSmooth short-term motion, which gradually accumulates errorThe encoder/odometry bridge publishes odom → base_link.
mapThe room reference, corrected when observations reveal driftSLAM supplies map → odom while mapping; a localizer supplies it during fixed-map navigation.

A loop-closure correction can move the robot’s estimated position in map while its odom motion remains continuous. This is why we keep both frames. Read the definitions of odom and map in REP 105, then explain which one you would use to inspect a sudden correction to the room map.

Check: point to the origin’s projection, explain where it lies vertically, and explain every arrow in your sketch. At this stage, having no map frame is expected; mapping creates it in Chapter 07. If a sensor orientation is uncertain, inspect its manufacturer’s frame diagram before entering a rotation.

Session 06B · Ask the wheels how far they went

45–60 minutes · Encoder logs, tape measure and clear floor · Result: a sign-and-scale check

Odometry estimates movement from measured motion. For our differential-drive rover, the inputs are the rotation of its left and right wheels. A motor command is an instruction; encoder counts are measurements of rotation. Neither establishes exactly how far the robot travelled when a wheel slips.

Use the count convention verified in Chapter 04. The proposed Pololu motor has a nominal 48 counts per motor revolution and a 46.85:1 reduction, giving approximately 2249 counts per output revolution under that stated counting convention. A controller that counts different edges changes the number you should use. Confirm it by turning the output through a known rotation and inspecting the reported counts; do not fix a fourfold error by “calibrating” the wheel diameter.

For each short update, let dL and dR be the left and right ground-travel estimates. With effective rolling radius r, counts per output revolution N, and wheel-centre separation b:

dL = 2π × rL × change_in_left_counts / NL
dR = 2π × rR × change_in_right_counts / NR
forward_step = (dL + dR) / 2
heading_change = (dR - dL) / b

These equations describe an ideal rolling model. For an illustrative step with dL = 0.10 m, dR = 0.12 m, and b = 0.20 m, the midpoint advances about 0.11 m and turns left by 0.10 rad, about 5.7°. A small-step integrator uses the heading halfway through that turn when updating x and y. The physical test tells us where the idealization stops matching the rover.

  1. With motor outputs disabled and commands inhibited, keep the controller and encoder electronics powered, roll the robot forward slowly, and log both counts. USB alone does not power the RoboClaw controller and encoders. After the bridge’s sign convention is applied, both distances should increase. Turning the chassis left should produce a positive yaw change.
  2. Mark a straight, unobstructed baseline and measure it. Drive slowly along it under manual control. Compare tape distance with the odometry distance, noting any curve or wheel slip.
  3. Repeat the run before changing a parameter. A repeatable scale error suggests the count convention or rolling radius. An inconsistent error suggests traction, loose hardware, missed counts, or measurement technique.
  4. Make a slow turn and compare the direction and approximate angle with floor marks. Only tune effective track width after straight-line signs and scale are credible.

Check: save raw counts, configured counts/revolution, wheel dimensions, tape distance, odometry distance, and observed turn direction. A consistent short-run estimate is enough to proceed to sensor fusion; there is no promise that wheel-only odometry will stay aligned around the room.

Session 06C · A return is evidence; a blank is a question

45–60 minutes · Live stationary lidar and optional depth images · Result: an annotated observation

The main mapping path uses a horizontal 2D lidar. Each valid return describes a range along a ray in the sensor’s scan plane. A tabletop above that plane and a small toy below it can both escape that slice. This is useful geometry for a first room map, with a specific field of view and specific blind spots.

  1. Inspect a stationary scan in RViz. Place a broad cardboard target in the scan plane and move it a measured distance. Compare the change in the displayed return.
  2. Identify a real wall, an object edge, and an area hidden behind an object. Label the drawing observed surface, observed free ray, or unknown.
  3. Lift a target out of the scan plane. Observe that its disappearance does not mean the whole volume is empty. Keep the initial test area clear of low objects and physically isolated from stairs.

Optional RGB-D adds a colour image and depth measurements. A point cloud is a collection of 3D samples produced from those measurements. For a calibrated, rectified image whose depth value Z is forward distance, a pixel (u, v) becomes a camera-frame point:

X = (u - cx) × Z / fx
Y = (v - cy) × Z / fy
Z = depth_in_metres

The calibration supplies the focal lengths fx, fy and image centre cx, cy. At Z = 2 m, a pixel 60 pixels to the right of centre with fx = 600 pixels lies 0.20 m right of the optical axis. This is an illustrative calculation, not a measurement from your camera. Invalid depth stays invalid; a zero depth value must not become an obstacle at the camera origin.

Check the driver’s image encoding and depth scale before converting values. Also check whether depth is registered to the colour image. Colour and depth pixels from different viewpoints cannot be paired merely because their arrays have the same shape. Camera optical frames conventionally point z forward, x right, and y down; the driver’s transforms connect that convention to the robot body.

Check: explain why an unseen patch behind a chair remains unknown. If you have RGB-D data, save one colour image, its matching depth image and calibration, with a marked pixel whose computed distance you can plausibly check. If the camera has not arrived, finish the lidar exercise and continue.

Session 06D · Make the frame chain tell the truth

45–60 minutes · Running acquisition stack · Result: a frame diagram and a checked live frame chain

Run these inspection commands in the sourced Jazzy environment. They do not create the robot bridge or sensor drivers; those programs must already be publishing their measured data.

ros2 topic list -t
ros2 topic echo /odom --once
ros2 run tf2_ros tf2_echo odom base_link
ros2 run tf2_tools view_frames

Inspect the generated frame diagram using the Nav2 transform guide as a reference. Each child frame needs one parent and one authoritative publisher for that connection. The camera driver may supply its own internal optical transforms; do not publish conflicting copies.
