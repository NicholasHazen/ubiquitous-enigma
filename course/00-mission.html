Design the parts. Make the wheels turn. Train a model. Build a measured map of one real room—and a path toward a robot that understands your home.

You already know how to build software. This book starts where the physical world makes that experience interesting: a printed hole that grips too tightly, a wheel that turns without travelling as far as expected, and a model that recognizes the carpet instead of the toy. We will make those effects observable, then change one thing and check what happened.

Our working vision is a home robot with spatial memory. It measures its surroundings, moves to selected places, and connects observations with context. Eventually you might ask it about a room or give it a useful task. The first finish line is smaller and concrete: a custom rover surveys one room, produces a checked metric map, completes a short waypoint trip, and records predictions from a model you trained.

The course is built around your actual starting point

You have fifteen-plus years of programming experience and use coding agents. You have designed single parts in Fusion, printed your own and downloaded designs, and wired Arduino LEDs. Assemblies, powered motion, SLAM, and training models are new. We explain those foundations directly and use your software skills to inspect the evidence.

Your bench has an X1 Carbon and normal measuring, soldering, and hand tools. Your Windows 11 machine has an RTX 4090; the GB10 Spark is on your LAN. We begin the learning code on CPU so you can verify it immediately, then enable acceleration deliberately. A modest native Ubuntu computer on the eventual rover handles sensor connections and local control. The desktop machines can process recorded data.

Most sessions target 45–60 minutes of active work. Prints, downloads, and hardware integration may take longer. Session estimates are planning aids, not deadlines. Optional evenings are available for curiosity; the core route fits into ordinary workday sessions.

The finish line has evidence

CapabilityWhat you show
Make and assembleA repeatable fit log, editable Fusion assembly, accessible fasteners, and a mechanically secure rover.
Move dependablyDocumented wheel direction/counts, manually measured motion, and demonstrated stopping after stale commands and lost communication.
Measure a roomA replayable recording, a saved map, and several independent reference distances with their errors reported.
NavigateA supervised short waypoint run in the bounded test area, including a deliberately placed obstacle and a cancel/stop test.
Train and interpretA dataset manifest, baseline and neural artifacts, validation decisions, reserved-test results, and examples of failures.
Remember an observationA queryable record containing the source image, time, map revision, capture pose, and model version.

For measuring, our purpose is understanding layout. A horizontal lidar slice cannot capture ceiling height or every surface, and a map that looks tidy does not establish dimensional accuracy. We will measure errors before deciding what the result is useful for.

A vision you can explain at work

Hypothesis: a mobile observer could help people keep useful, current knowledge of their home. The first research question is whether it can produce trustworthy spatial observations. Later, we ask whether those observations answer a recurring question well enough that someone uses the system again. A plausible prototype and demonstrated customer value are separate forms of evidence.

The project does not have a fixed budget yet. Chapter 04 sets out a staged reference architecture and the checks required before each purchase. You can complete the first data and fit experiments with equipment already at hand.

Checkpoint: write the first finish line in your project notes and name the room and object you will use. The complete robot and its printed chassis have not been physically built or verified for this edition; the book distinguishes executable desktop exercises from hardware integration work.
