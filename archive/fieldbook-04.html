The reading sequence for your first model

Start with statistical learning and regression to give fitting and evaluation concrete meanings. Then work through the joint-data contract and simple predictors. Use PyTorch’s training workflow when you reach the small neural network, and finish with the locked evaluation. Read a section when its experiment needs it; you do not need to finish a textbook first.

Bring in mathematics as it becomes useful: vectors describe inputs and outputs; a mean and variance describe data; a derivative describes how a small parameter change affects a loss. The model lesson introduces those roles before asking you to use them. Reinforcement learning comes after you can train, evaluate, save, and reload a simpler model.

Use your coding agent without outsourcing the experiment

Give the agent a bounded implementation contract: input schema and units, allowed dependencies, expected artifacts, and the checks that must pass. Let it scaffold collectors, plots, and test harnesses. Keep the physical limits, dataset split, baseline, and evaluation criteria under your review.

Before a run, write a prediction and the evidence that would change your mind. Afterward, inspect actual traces and error cases. A plausible explanation from an agent does not establish that a model learned a useful relationship.

Chapter 01 · Plastic becomes a robot part

Print a home
for two motors.

A practical printing path for your Bambu Lab X1 Carbon: start with a fit strip and a rover deck, then learn to design joints and mounts that fit on purpose.

A robot chassis is a place to hold things in the right relationship. We’ll give two motors a flat deck, let a ball caster support the front, and leave the top open so you can reach every wire. Cable ties make the first revision forgiving. You can cut one and move a motor without throwing away the whole print.

The printable starter parts below are included in this HTML file. They are dimensioned design examples, checked as closed meshes; they have not been physically printed or fitted to your parts. That is why our first task is small enough to hold between two fingers.

Start here · Give your X1 Carbon a repeatable baseline

10 minutes · Bambu Studio, plain PLA, your installed build plate · Win: a saved starting recipe

Your X1 Carbon can handle this rover. The useful new skill is deciding which surfaces must fit, then checking those surfaces. Automatic calibration helps the printing process; it does not certify that your shaft fits your hole.

  1. Select Bambu Lab X1 Carbon and the nozzle actually fitted. This guide assumes the standard 0.4 mm nozzle. Select the plate type printed on the plate you are using. Keep the plate-specific temperature and surface-preparation instructions; there is no single bed-temperature recipe for every X1 plate.
  2. Use one plain, opaque PLA spool you already print successfully. Select its matching filament preset, or Generic PLA for an ordinary third-party PLA. Keep that spool for the fit experiments. Leave the filament’s existing flow ratio and temperature settings alone for the first test.
  3. Start from the 0.20 mm Standard process for that printer/nozzle. Set three wall loops, four top/bottom shell layers, and 20% infill. Keep supports off for our flat parts and the normal print-speed mode. Save a copy as X1C-PLA-robot-baseline.
  4. For a readable baseline, set manual X-Y hole compensation and X-Y contour compensation to 0 mm; leave Auto circle contour-hole compensation off if your Studio version offers it. Keep and record the preset’s elephant-foot setting. These settings affect different things; we will diagnose before changing them.
  5. Follow Studio’s PLA chamber/door advice for the selected filament and plate. If it warns about heat buildup, vent the front door and/or top cover as directed before starting. Keep bed leveling enabled and follow any calibration compatibility prompts rather than forcing an unsupported plate/material combination.

Ready means: the preview names your actual nozzle, plate, and PLA; the saved project records the settings. Keep a native Fusion design for editable geometry and a Bambu Studio 3MF project for this print arrangement. An STL alone does not remember your slicing recipe.

Bench 01A · Print a tiny question

15–25 minutes active work + printing · PLA, slicer, one M3 screw · Win: know which slot fits

“M3” means a metric screw with a nominal 3 mm thread diameter. A 3 mm gap in CAD may print smaller than 3 mm. Our strip has four open slots: 2.8, 3.0, 3.2, and 3.4 mm, increasing from left to right when the slots face away from you. We are testing slot clearance, not certifying the size of round holes.

Download the clearance strip · STL

  1. Save clearance-strip.stl in your project folder and open it in your slicer. Set units to millimetres if asked. It should measure 40 × 20 × 4 mm. If it is much larger or smaller, fix the units before slicing.
  2. Place the broad 40 × 20 mm face on the bed. Use the X1C baseline you just saved. Preview the first layer, the slots, and the top: all four openings must remain visible. Print it.
  3. Let it cool, remove it, and label the slots 2.8, 3.0, 3.2, 3.4 with a fine pen. Hold the threaded part of your M3 screw beside each slot and slide it in gently. Do not screw it into the plastic.
  4. Record the smallest slot that accepts the screw without force. Try your 2.5 mm cable tie in the 3.4 mm slot too.

You should see: at least one slot accepts the screw, and the cable tie passes through the widest slot. The narrow slots may be deliberately too tight.

If all four are tight: check for a flared first layer. Try the screw from the upper face. If that works, the bottom edge is the problem: use the diagnosis table to check elephant-foot compensation and reprint this tiny strip. If both faces are tight, measure the outside width against 40 mm and follow that same table before printing the deck. Don’t force the fastener and call it a fit.

Make the measurement useful

Clean the caliper jaws, close them gently, and zero them. Measure the strip’s 40 mm outside span three times, lifting and replacing the jaws each time. Keep them square and use light pressure. Compare near the upper face and near the bed face; a wider bottom suggests a local first-layer bulge. A display that shows hundredths of a millimetre does not guarantee that accuracy.

Use the actual screw as the fit test; tiny holes and thread peaks are awkward caliper targets. Record the result as “slides freely,” “needs force,” or “too loose for this job.” Clearance is intentional space. Tolerance is the acceptable variation in a dimension. “This motor must not shift in its mount” is the requirement that makes those numbers useful.

Download the fit log CSV or copy its columns into your notebook. Three readings of one object check your measuring consistency. Before committing to a critical fit, print three specimens with the same saved settings and compare their ranges; that also samples print-to-print variation.

Prediction: if you double the gap, does the printed wall get stronger? No—the gap changes clearance, and it also removes material. Fit and strength are related design constraints, but they are different questions.

Bench 01B · Give the wheels a home

20 minutes active work + printing · Same PLA profile · Win: a flat deck and adjustable caster height

The deck is 120 mm wide, 160 mm long, and 4 mm thick. Its rear is narrowed to 90 mm to clear the wheels. Four narrow slots hold the motors underneath; each tie returns around the outer edge of the deck. Four central slots help strap down the battery. The two short slots near the front are for the caster. The spacer pieces go between caster and deck so you can bring all three contacts onto the floor.

Download the rover deck · STL

Download each spacer. Your slicer can duplicate the parts:

  • 8 mm spacer — print three.
  • 4 mm spacer — print one.
  • 2 mm spacer — print two.
  • 1 mm spacer — print two.
  1. Slice the deck flat with the same successful PLA settings. Confirm its 120 × 160 mm footprint fits the printable area without clipping. Inspect the layer preview: all slots must stay open. Print and cool it.
  2. Print the spacer set with its broad faces flat. Each piece is 24 × 24 mm with two slots. Keep the thin spacers together in a labelled bag.
  3. Put the unpowered motors underneath, near the narrow rear corners as drawn. Their metal motor cans point toward the front; their output shafts point outwards. Put both axle centres on a line 35 mm forward of the rear edge. Each motor body centre is about 40 mm to its side of the deck centreline. Run each cable tie through an inner slot, across the top to the nearby outside edge, then around the motor body and back to the tie head. Use two ties and a thin foam pad per motor. Keep straps away from shafts, wires, and motor terminals; slide a strap along its slot to clear protrusions. Keep ties loose for now.
  4. Fit the rubber tyre to each wheel, then press the wheel onto its matching motor shaft and use its supplied screw. Support the gearbox while fitting it. Slide the motors until the wheels clear the deck by at least 2 mm, then tighten the ties enough to stop movement without crushing the motor housing. Check the tyre-to-deck gap visually; turn a wheel gently only if its gearbox permits it. Do not force a stiff gearbox. Chapter 04 checks free rotation under motor power.
  5. Put the rover on a flat surface. Hold the deck level. Measure the gap from the front underside to the floor; subtract the caster’s 16 mm height. Build that thickness from the spacers, to the nearest millimetre. For a measured 42 mm gap, use 26 mm of spacers: 8 + 8 + 8 + 2.
  6. Fit the caster beneath that stack using two M3 bolts inserted from the caster side up through the spacers and deck, with washers and nuts above the deck. Start with the 45 mm bolts. Check that enough thread emerges to engage each nut fully and that bolt heads cannot touch the ball. Spare thread points upward. Use a shorter bolt from the kit if necessary. Tighten gently; thin spacers should sit flat.

You should see: both tyres and the caster touch the surface; the deck is approximately level; wheels turn without rubbing; neither motor slides when you lightly push its body. Nothing is powered.

If the motor rocks: move the foam under a broad part of its plastic case and retighten both ties. Keep straps away from the axle and exposed metal motor terminals. If the caster hangs in the air: add spacer thickness. If it lifts a drive wheel: remove a little. Do this fit check before adding electronics.

Bench 01C · Make the tiny strip yours in Fusion

20–30 minutes · Fusion · Win: change one dimension deliberately

You have a printable part to keep the build moving. Now we’ll remake the small strip in Fusion so you own one piece of the design. A sketch is its flat outline. Extrude gives that outline thickness.

  1. Create a new design and set document units to millimetres. Create a component named clearance_strip. Start a sketch on the XY plane.
  2. Draw a two-point rectangle with one corner at the origin. Use the sketch dimension tool to set it to 40 mm along X and 20 mm along Y. Finish the sketch, then extrude the rectangle 4 mm.
  3. Start a sketch on the top face. Draw four rectangular notches extending 6 mm inward from the back edge. Place their centres at X = 5, 15, 25, and 35 mm from the left edge. Dimension their widths to 2.8, 3.0, 3.2, and 3.4 mm.
  4. Finish the sketch. Select the four notch profiles and extrude them through the strip with operation Cut. Save as clearance-strip-v1.
  5. Edit the widest slot dimension from 3.4 to 3.6 mm. Confirm only that slot changes. Save a new version and export a mesh in millimetres. You do not need another print today unless your first strip failed the fit test.

You should see: a 40 × 20 × 4 mm strip with four slots, and a history you can edit. You changed fit without redrawing the whole part.

If Extrude fills a notch instead of removing it: check that you selected the notch’s closed profile and that the operation is Cut. If a profile will not select, zoom into its corners and close the sketch gaps. Ask AI to explain that one failed step using a screenshot; keep the part small.

Name the dimension you expect to change

Open Modify → Change Parameters. Add slot_width = 3.4 mm and edit the widest notch’s dimension to use slot_width. Change the parameter to 3.6 mm and watch only that notch change. That is your first reusable fit decision. Autodesk’s video on parameters and constrained sketches shows the workflow: stop when you can drive one sketch dimension by name, then repeat it on your strip.

For a future pocket around a measured 20.0 mm-wide part, use part_width = 20 mm, clearance_each_side = 0.2 mm, and pocket_width = part_width + 2 * clearance_each_side. The resulting 20.4 mm pocket leaves 0.2 mm on each side in the design. Print a short slice of that pocket before making a full mount. The 0.2 mm is a trial value, not an X1 Carbon guarantee.

When exporting a round feature from Fusion, use a sufficiently fine mesh and inspect its outline in Studio. A visibly faceted hole is a geometry/export problem. Save the source design and verify millimetres and overall dimensions after import; do not resize the whole robot to repair one tight hole.

02A · Fit the shield once

A shield is a circuit board that plugs into the Uno's rows of sockets. The #1438 product is currently called v3; the guide and Arduino library retain the V2 name. This path uses that product with an Uno R3. An old v1 shield or an L293D lookalike needs different instructions.

  2. If the headers are loose, complete Adafruit's standard-header assembly sequence now. Use the included straight headers. Current kits normally have the screw terminals fitted already, so inspect your actual board before repeating those older steps. Keep both boards unpowered.

The three electrical ideas you need today

Voltage is a compatibility requirement. Our TT motors are specified for 3–6 V, while this driver needs at least 4.5 V. Four charged NiMH cells give us an overlapping operating range; they do not stay there forever. Once the rover can run on its stand, we will check that the pack remains at or above 4.5 V while the motors turn.

Current rises when a motor works harder. The TT motor page supports this driver family but warns that stalled motors can draw high current. The shield is rated at 1.2 A per motor bridge, with a much shorter peak allowance. This combination is for a light rover rolling freely. A trapped wheel is a reason to switch off, not a test of strength. A low PWM setting does not guarantee a safe stall current.

The driver does the electrical lifting. The Uno sends small control signals. The shield switches power from the pack into the motors. Keeping these jobs separate helps the program keep running when a motor starts.

Bench note · what “4.8 V” and “1.2 A” actually tell you

The pack's nominal voltage is a convenient label; the measured voltage changes with charge and load. A driver's current rating is a limit under stated thermal conditions, not a current it forces through the motor. Do not substitute a high-current battery and assume the driver has become stronger. Electrical input power is voltage times current: 5 V at 0.3 A is 1.5 W. Some becomes motion; some becomes heat.

A meter may miss a very brief voltage dip. If the Uno repeatedly resets when motors start, disconnect motor power and recheck the removed jumper, solder joints, USB connection and pack condition before changing code.

// Arduino Uno R3 + Adafruit Motor Shield #1438 (v2/v3).
// Install "Adafruit Motor Shield V2 Library" with its dependencies.
// Left motor M1, right motor M2. Calibrate wire polarity on a stand.
#include <Wire.h>
#include <Adafruit_MotorShield.h>

Adafruit_MotorShield shield;
Adafruit_DCMotor *leftMotor = shield.getMotor(1);
Adafruit_DCMotor *rightMotor = shield.getMotor(2);
const int DRIVE_PWM = 90;       // Effort, not a measured wheel speed.
const int TURN_PWM = 70;
const unsigned long TIMEOUT_MS = 350;
unsigned long lastCommandMs = 0;
char requested = 'X';
char applied = '?';
bool shieldReady = false;

void setMotor(Adafruit_DCMotor *motor, int demand) {
  if (demand == 0) {
    motor->setSpeed(0);
    motor->run(RELEASE);       // Coast; this is not an active brake.
  } else {
    motor->setSpeed(abs(demand));
    motor->run(demand > 0 ? FORWARD : BACKWARD);
  }
}

void applyCommand(char command) {
  int left = 0;
  int right = 0;
  switch (command) {
    case 'F': left = DRIVE_PWM; right = DRIVE_PWM; break;
    case 'B': left = -DRIVE_PWM; right = -DRIVE_PWM; break;
    case 'L': left = -TURN_PWM; right = TURN_PWM; break;
    case 'R': left = TURN_PWM; right = -TURN_PWM; break;
    case '1': left = DRIVE_PWM; break;  // Left wheel only, forward.
    case '2': right = DRIVE_PWM; break; // Right wheel only, forward.
    default: break;                    // X means both demands stay 0.
  }
  setMotor(leftMotor, left);
  setMotor(rightMotor, right);
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(115200);
  shieldReady = shield.begin();
  if (!shieldReady) {
    Serial.println(F("SHIELD NOT FOUND. Power off; check headers."));
    return;
  }
  applyCommand('X');
  applied = 'X';
  Serial.println(F("READY. F B L R X; bench tests 1 and 2."));
}

void loop() {
  if (!shieldReady) return;

  // Read at most 8 bytes per visit, so timeout checking keeps running.
  for (byte count = 0; count < 8 && Serial.available(); ++count) {
    char incoming = Serial.read();
    if (incoming == '\r' || incoming == '\n' || incoming == ' ') continue;
    switch (incoming) {
      case 'F': case 'B': case 'L': case 'R': case '1': case '2':
        requested = incoming;
        lastCommandMs = millis();
        break;
      case 'X':
        requested = 'X';
        break;
      default:
        requested = 'X';       // Unknown input stops; never refreshes time.
        break;
    }
  }

  if (requested != 'X' &&
      (unsigned long)(millis() - lastCommandMs) >= TIMEOUT_MS) {
    requested = 'X';
    Serial.println(F("STOP timeout"));
  }
  if (requested != applied) {
    applyCommand(requested);
    applied = requested;
    Serial.print(F("Command: "));
    Serial.println(applied);
  }
}

Review three firmware contracts

Open the sketch and find DRIVE_PWM. That number sets motor effort on a scale from 0 to 255. A setting of 90 is not 90 RPM, and half the number does not promise half the ground speed. Load, friction and pack voltage all get a vote.

Review the freshness contract around lastCommandMs. A valid movement command refreshes it; elapsed time in loop() eventually changes requested to 'X'. The Uno enforces that timeout locally even when the laptop stops making progress. A successful serial write is evidence of sending a request, not evidence of motion.

Finally, find RELEASE. The driver stops driving the motor, so a moving wheel can coast. The 350 ms limit is a software deadline, not a stopping-distance guarantee. The pack switch also matters: a timeout cannot rescue a completely hung controller or a broken connection to the driver.

  3. Send 1 once. Only the left wheel should turn briefly, then coast to rest. At the top of that wheel, the tire should travel toward the robot's front; that rotation would propel the chassis forward.
  4. If the left wheel turns the other way, switch the pack off, unplug USB and swap that motor's two wires at M1. Reconnect, wait for READY, switch on and repeat 1. Do not change code and wiring at the same time.
  5. Send 2 and perform the same check for the right wheel at M2. The motors face opposite sides, so their wire orders need not match. What matters is that both have the same meaning of forward.
  6. Send F, wait for stop, then send B. Both wheels should agree in each case. Send L: left wheel backward, right wheel forward. Send R: the opposite. Send X to finish.

A stop test you can see

With the wheels raised, send F once and then take your hands off the keyboard. The motor command should be released after about 350 ms and the wheels should coast to rest. Watch the wheel as well as STOP timeout. Repeat this three times. Do not proceed to the floor if any run keeps driving.

Next send FX in one Serial Monitor submission. The final command is stop, so the sketch should finish that input batch stopped rather than hold forward. This checks the stop command; the silence test above checks the timeout. Later, the camera script will let us interrupt a continuous stream.

04C · Check the pack under this small load

Turn the pack off. With the multimeter still in DC-voltage mode, secure its probes or insulated test clips across the motor-power + and − terminals, with no exposed metal able to bridge them. Keep leads clear of the wheels. Switch on and send short F commands, reading the voltage while the wheels turn. If it drops below 4.5 V, switch off and recharge the NiMH cells before continuing. If you cannot secure the probes without holding them near moving parts, turn off and use insulated clips; do not juggle loose probes and a moving robot.

The meter may miss a brief dip; this is a useful check, not a complete picture of electrical noise. Remove the probes with power off before the floor run.

Later upgrade: encoders and automatic speed correction

Leave this closed until your camera rover works. The main build needs no encoder purchase or PID tuning. When you want repeatable commanded speeds, replace the drivetrain with compatible encoder-equipped motors and select a driver for their current demands. That is a separate hardware-and-firmware upgrade; the two-wire TT motors cannot acquire position feedback through software.

After verifying the number of counts per wheel revolution, convert counts to speed. For a radius of 0.03 metres, 360 counts per wheel revolution, and 18 counts in 0.10 seconds, the wheel turned 0.05 revolutions at 0.5 revolutions per second: 30 rpm. Under rolling without slip, its ground-speed estimate is 0.5 × 2π × 0.03 = 0.0942 m/s. Measure elapsed time rather than assuming the loop ran on schedule.

A feedback controller compares a requested speed with that estimate. Begin with a proportional correction, output = Kp × (requested − measured), limit the output, and plot what happens. Add an integral term only after you understand the remaining steady error; limit its accumulation when the output saturates. Clear it on a stop. Brian Douglas's What Is PID Control? is the video to watch when you reach this upgrade.

A future motion interface can accept forward speed v and left-turn rate ω. For wheel-track separation b, target wheel speeds are left = v − ωb/2 and right = v + ωb/2. Use metres and seconds consistently. If a target exceeds the wheel-speed limit, scale both targets together. This requires a new, explicitly versioned command format and speed feedback; the existing F/B/L/R/X protocol does not carry these quantities.

Even matching encoder speeds cannot guarantee a straight ground path. Tyre diameter, slip, alignment, and caster behavior still matter. A robot can produce a lovely graph while driving into a chair leg.

"""Blue-card steering demo. Dry run unless --port is supplied; Space arms.
Requires Python 3.11+, opencv-python and pyserial. Firmware: rover.ino.
No forward motion is sent. Test raised wheels before a tethered floor trial.
"""
import argparse
import threading
import time

import cv2
import numpy as np
import serial

LOWER_BLUE = (90, 100, 60)       # OpenCV HSV: H 0..179, S/V 0..255
UPPER_BLUE = (130, 255, 255)
DEADBAND = 0.12                  # Fraction of half the image width
MAX_FRAME_AGE = 0.25             # Since capture completed, not sensor exposure
PERIOD = 0.10                    # At most 10 command updates per second


class LatestCamera:
    """Keep reading so the control loop uses the latest delivered image."""
    def __init__(self, index):
        self.cap = cv2.VideoCapture(index)
        if not self.cap.isOpened():
            self.cap.release()
            raise RuntimeError("Cannot open camera. Close other camera apps; try --camera 1.")
        self.cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_FRAME_WIDTH, 640)
        self.cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_FRAME_HEIGHT, 480)
        self.cap.set(cv2.CAP_PROP_BUFFERSIZE, 1)  # Best effort; backend may ignore it
        self.lock = threading.Lock()
        self.stop = threading.Event()
        self.frame = None
        self.sequence = 0
        self.stamp = 0.0
        self.failed = False
        self.thread = threading.Thread(target=self.read_loop, daemon=True)
        self.thread.start()

    def read_loop(self):
        try:
            while not self.stop.is_set():
                ok, frame = self.cap.read()
                if not ok:
                    with self.lock:
                        self.failed = True
                    break
                with self.lock:
                    self.frame = frame
                    self.sequence += 1
                    self.stamp = time.monotonic()
        except Exception:
            with self.lock:
                self.failed = True
        finally:
            self.cap.release()

    def snapshot(self):
        with self.lock:
            frame = None if self.frame is None else self.frame.copy()
            return self.sequence, self.stamp, frame, self.failed

    def close(self):
        self.stop.set()
        self.thread.join(timeout=0.5)  # A stuck camera must not delay the stop command


def locate_target(frame):
    """Return a command, normalized error, and mask; annotate frame in place."""
    hsv = cv2.cvtColor(frame, cv2.COLOR_BGR2HSV)
    mask = cv2.inRange(hsv, LOWER_BLUE, UPPER_BLUE)
    contours, _ = cv2.findContours(mask, cv2.RETR_EXTERNAL, cv2.CHAIN_APPROX_SIMPLE)
    height, width = frame.shape[:2]
    for x in (int(width * (1 - DEADBAND) / 2),
              int(width * (1 + DEADBAND) / 2)):
        cv2.line(frame, (x, 0), (x, height - 1), (0, 200, 255), 1)
    if not contours:
        return "X", None, mask
    target = max(contours, key=cv2.contourArea)
    area = cv2.contourArea(target) / (width * height)
    moments = cv2.moments(target)
    if not 0.003 <= area <= 0.70 or moments["m00"] == 0:
        return "X", None, mask
    cx = moments["m10"] / moments["m00"]
    cy = moments["m01"] / moments["m00"]
    error = (cx - width / 2) / (width / 2)
    cv2.drawContours(frame, [target], -1, (0, 255, 0), 2)
    cv2.circle(frame, (int(cx), int(cy)), 5, (0, 255, 0), -1)
    command = "X" if abs(error) <= DEADBAND else ("L" if error < 0 else "R")
    return command, error, mask


def main():
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser(description=__doc__)
    parser.add_argument("--camera", type=int, default=0)
    parser.add_argument("--port", help="Arduino port, e.g. COM4 or /dev/ttyACM0")
    args = parser.parse_args()
    connection = None
    camera = None
    armed = False
    last_sequence = -1
    armed_at = 0.0
    try:
        if args.port:
            connection = serial.Serial(args.port, 115200, timeout=0.1, write_timeout=0.2)
            time.sleep(2.0)  # Uno normally resets when its serial port opens
            connection.write(b"X")
        camera = LatestCamera(args.camera)
        print("Click the image: Space = arm/disarm; q or Esc = stop and exit.")
        print("LIVE serial connected; starts disarmed." if connection else "DRY RUN: no motor commands sent.")
        while True:
            started = time.monotonic()
            sequence, stamp, frame, failed = camera.snapshot()
            fresh = (frame is not None and not failed and sequence != last_sequence
                     and started - stamp <= MAX_FRAME_AGE)
            last_sequence = sequence
            wanted, error = "X", None
            if frame is None:
                frame = np.zeros((480, 640, 3), dtype=np.uint8)
            if fresh:
                wanted, error, mask = locate_target(frame)
            else:
                mask = np.zeros(frame.shape[:2], dtype=np.uint8)
                armed = False  # Camera hiccup needs a deliberate re-arm
            # Two tenths turning, three tenths stopped: small supervised nudges.
            pulse_on = ((started - armed_at) % 0.5) < 0.2
            sent = wanted if armed and fresh and pulse_on else "X"
            mode = "LIVE" if connection else "DRY RUN"
            status = f"{mode} | {'ARMED' if armed else 'DISARMED'} | want {wanted} | send {sent}"
            if not fresh:

                sent = "X"  # Every arm/disarm transition first requests stop

Chapter 07 · A joint on the bench

Make a tiny robot arm

One servo. One pointer. Three places it can point. We’ll make that work before asking a whole creature to cooperate.

The rover’s motors keep turning while you feed them power. A positional servo has a different job: move toward a requested position and resist being moved away from it. Put a little pointer on one and you have the beginning of a camera mount, a waving arm, or a snake joint. Today’s mechanism can be small enough to lose beneath a coffee mug.

Today’s win: your pointer visits three positions, returns near its center, and stops receiving commands when its timer expires. Budget two short bench sessions, with a print between them. The first session works without a printed part.

Show the complete joint sketch
#include <Servo.h>

// Arduino Uno R3 + one positional micro servo. External regulated 5 V power.
// Serial Monitor: 115200 baud. A = arm, L/C/R = 75/90/105, X = stop pulses.
// These are nominal command angles, not measured shaft angles.
Servo joint;
const byte SERVO_PIN = 9;
const unsigned long COMMAND_LEASE_MS = 5000;
bool armed = false;
float commandAngle = 90.0f;
float targetAngle = 90.0f;
unsigned long lastCommand = 0;
unsigned long lastTick = 0;

void disarm() {
  joint.detach();                 // Stops signal pulses; does NOT cut servo power.
  digitalWrite(SERVO_PIN, LOW);
  armed = false;
  Serial.println(F("OFF: unplug servo power before handling."));
}

void setup() {
  pinMode(SERVO_PIN, OUTPUT);
  digitalWrite(SERVO_PIN, LOW);
  Serial.begin(115200);
  Serial.println(F("READY: A arms at center. L C R move. X stops pulses."));
}

void loop() {
  unsigned long now = millis();
  for (byte n = 0; n < 16 && Serial.available(); ++n) {
    char c = Serial.read();
    if (c == '\r' || c == '\n' || c == ' ') continue;
    if (c == 'X') {
      disarm();
    } else if (c == 'A') {
      if (!armed) {
        commandAngle = 90.0f;
        joint.attach(SERVO_PIN, 1000, 2000);
        joint.write(90);          // Initial centering can jump. Start with horn off.
        armed = true;
      }
      targetAngle = 90.0f;
      lastCommand = now;
      lastTick = now;
    } else if (c == 'L' || c == 'C' || c == 'R') {
      if (armed) {
        targetAngle = c == 'L' ? 75.0f : c == 'R' ? 105.0f : 90.0f;
        lastCommand = now;
      } else {
        Serial.println(F("Send A first, with the mechanism clear."));
      }
    } else {
      disarm();                   // Unknown input never creates motion.
    }
  }
  if (!armed) return;
  if (now - lastCommand >= COMMAND_LEASE_MS) {
    disarm();
    return;
  }
  if (now - lastTick >= 20) {
    lastTick = now;
    // At most 0.4 nominal degrees each 20 ms: 20 degrees/second.
    float error = targetAngle - commandAngle;
    commandAngle += constrain(error, -0.4f, 0.4f);
    joint.write((int)(commandAngle + 0.5f));
  }
}

The sketch adds a small amount to the requested angle every 20 milliseconds. That makes ordinary moves gentler. Its first A command still has to establish a center without knowing where the shaft was, so initial centering can jump. The values 75, 90, and 105 are nominal library commands, not measurements of the real shaft. The Arduino Servo reference explains how its angle commands map to pulse widths.

Why a longer arm changes the job

Before adding a camera or a gripper, use the lab below. Picture an arm lifting a 100 g object held 5 cm from its shaft. Gravity needs about 0.10 × 9.81 × 0.05 = 0.049 N·m of turning effort, called torque. Put that same object twice as far away and its torque contribution doubles. A real link’s own weight adds to the load too.

That calculation is for holding still with a horizontal lever. Starting, stopping, rubbing bearings, and impacts add demands. A listed stall torque describes the point where the motor cannot turn; it is not a comfortable load to hold indefinitely. For this chapter, keep the card pointer and no payload. The next build keeps each segment supported by wheels so the servo mainly turns it sideways.

Build a little
wiggle machine

Three joints, four short segments, and a bend that travels from one end to the other. Our first job is a reliable wiggle. Our next is finding out whether it goes anywhere.

The appealing part happens quickly: coordinated joints make an object look rather more alive than its pile of parts suggested. The floor is less easily impressed. A body can waggle all afternoon and finish exactly where it started. We’ll build a small wheel-supported creature, give it one fixed wave, and measure what the floor lets it do.

This build’s finish line: a supported three-joint body performs the same bounded movement three times, and you can say how far it traveled—or show exactly what prevented travel. Net movement is an experimental result, not a guaranteed outcome of this unbuilt starting design. A reliable joint is already a completed project; this chapter is your first locomotion investigation.

Session 1 · Make one segment that rolls the right way

  1. Start the deck. In Fusion make an 80 × 50 × 3 mm plate, with x along its length and y across it. Its center is x = 0, y = 0. What you should see: four rims touching the surface, a deck clear of the floor, and a noticeable difference between rolling and sliding. Swiveling caster wheels would turn to follow a sideways shove, so they defeat the contact behavior we are trying to create here.

Show the complete crawler sketch
#include <Servo.h>
#include <math.h>

// Arduino Uno R3; three independently powered positional servos.
// Serial: 115200. A arms; 1/2/3 test a joint; G runs a 12-second wave.
// B reverses wave phase; L/R apply +/-3 nominal degrees of curvature.
// X stops pulses. Physical DC disconnect is the power-off control.
const byte COUNT = 3;
const byte PINS[COUNT] = {9, 10, 11};
const int SIGN[COUNT] = {1, 1, 1}; // Change an entry to -1 after direction test.
const float AMPLITUDE = 12.0f;    // Nominal command degrees, not measured angles.
const float FREQUENCY = 0.25f;    // Cycles per second.
const float PHASE = 1.04719755f;  // 60 degrees expressed in radians.
const unsigned long TRIAL_MS = 12000;
Servo servos[COUNT];
float commandAngles[COUNT] = {90, 90, 90};
enum Mode { OFF, HOLD, TEST, RUN };
Mode mode = OFF;
unsigned long started = 0;
unsigned long lastTick = 0;
unsigned long holdDuration = 5000;
int testJoint = 0;
float bias = 0.0f;
float phaseSign = 1.0f;

void disarm() {
  for (byte i = 0; i < COUNT; ++i) {
    servos[i].detach();
    digitalWrite(PINS[i], LOW);
  }
  mode = OFF;
  Serial.println(F("OFF: disconnect servo power before handling."));
}

void holdCenter(unsigned long now, unsigned long duration) {
  mode = HOLD;
  started = now;
  holdDuration = duration;
}

void setup() {
  for (byte i = 0; i < COUNT; ++i) {
    pinMode(PINS[i], OUTPUT);
    digitalWrite(PINS[i], LOW);
  }
  Serial.begin(115200);
  Serial.println(F("READY: A, then 1/2/3 or G. X stops pulses."));
}

void loop() {
  unsigned long now = millis();
  for (byte n = 0; n < 16 && Serial.available(); ++n) {
    char c = Serial.read();
    if (c == '\r' || c == '\n' || c == ' ') continue;
    if (c == 'X') {
      disarm();
    } else if (c == 'A') {
      if (mode == OFF) {
        for (byte i = 0; i < COUNT; ++i) {
          commandAngles[i] = 90.0f;
          servos[i].attach(PINS[i], 1000, 2000);
          servos[i].write(90);    // Can jump at arming: support the whole body.
        }
      }
      holdCenter(now, 5000);
      lastTick = now;
    } else if (c >= '1' && c <= '3' && mode == HOLD) {
      testJoint = c - '1';
      mode = TEST;
      started = now;
    } else if ((c == 'G' || c == 'B' || c == 'L' || c == 'R') && mode == HOLD) {
      bias = c == 'L' ? 3.0f : c == 'R' ? -3.0f : 0.0f;
      phaseSign = c == 'B' ? -1.0f : 1.0f;
      mode = RUN;
      started = now;
      Serial.println(F("TRIAL: 12 seconds; X stops early."));
    } else {
      // Commands out of sequence also stop. Use A to set up each new run.
      disarm();
    }
  }
  if (mode == OFF) return;
  unsigned long elapsed = now - started;
  if ((mode == TEST && elapsed >= 2000) || (mode == RUN && elapsed >= TRIAL_MS)) {
    holdCenter(now, 3000);
    elapsed = 0;
    Serial.println(F("CENTERING: pulses end in 3 seconds."));
  }
  if (mode == HOLD && elapsed >= holdDuration) {
    disarm();
    return;
  }
  if (now - lastTick < 20) return;
  lastTick = now;
  for (byte i = 0; i < COUNT; ++i) {
    float relative = 0.0f;
    if (mode == TEST && i == testJoint) relative = 8.0f;
    if (mode == RUN) {
      float seconds = elapsed / 1000.0f;
      // One-second fade at each end avoids an abrupt wave start or stop.
      float envelope = min(1.0f, min(seconds, (TRIAL_MS - elapsed) / 1000.0f));
      relative = envelope * (bias + AMPLITUDE * sinf(6.28318531f * FREQUENCY * seconds + phaseSign * i * PHASE));
    }
    float target = constrain(90.0f + SIGN[i] * relative, 75.0f, 105.0f);
    commandAngles[i] += constrain(target - commandAngles[i], -0.6f, 0.6f);
    servos[i].write((int)(commandAngles[i] + 0.5f));
  }
}

The small idea inside the wiggle

Every joint repeats the same motion, but starts at a different point in the cycle. The starting point is its phase. With different phases, the bend appears to travel. The sketch uses a nominal amplitude of 12°, one cycle every four seconds, and 60° of phase between neighbors. It fades the wave in and out and limits how quickly each command changes.

The lab displays five joints to make the pattern easier to follow; your starter body has three. Keep the firmware’s small default commands for the physical trials. In the lab, first change only the phase to zero. All joints then bend together. Restore the phase and the traveling pattern returns. The sketch is showing joint geometry; it contains no wheels, friction, or prediction of real travel. You can discover the shape here and still need the tabletop to discover the propulsion.

Put the wave into one line of mathematics

qᵢ(t) = b + A sin(2πft + iφ). Here A is amplitude, f is cycles per second, φ is phase difference in radians, and b is a curvature bias. Joint index i starts at zero. Our code converts the resulting nominal relative angle to a servo command around 90. The largest speed requested by an unmodified fixed wave is 2πfA; faster frequency therefore asks the joint to move faster even when its endpoints stay the same. The fade and final angle/rate limits also affect the actual command.

"""Fieldbook joint experiment. Writes CSV and SVG; never connects to hardware."""
import argparse
import csv
import math
from pathlib import Path
import xml.etree.ElementTree as ET

import mujoco

HERE = Path(__file__).resolve().parent
TARGET = 0.4  # radians, about 23 degrees
SPEED_WEIGHT_S = 0.03  # preference, not a measured physical constant


def run_trial(ramp_s=0.0, mass_kg=0.10, target_rad=TARGET):
    if not (0 <= ramp_s <= 1 and 0.02 <= mass_kg <= 0.5 and 0 < target_rad <= 0.5):
        raise ValueError("Use ramp 0..1 s, mass 0.02..0.5 kg, and target above 0 through 0.5 rad")
    xml = ET.parse(HERE / "joint.xml")
    xml.find(".//geom[@name='link_shape']").set("mass", str(mass_kg))
    model = mujoco.MjModel.from_xml_string(ET.tostring(xml.getroot(), encoding="unicode"))
    data = mujoco.MjData(model)  # a fresh, stationary joint for every trial
    rows = []
    command = 0.0
    for step in range(1000):  # 2 simulated seconds; physics at 500 Hz
        desired = target_rad if data.time >= 0.2 - 1e-9 else 0.0
        if step % 10 == 0:  # update command at 50 Hz
            fraction = 1.0 if ramp_s == 0 else max(0.0, min(1.0, (data.time - 0.2) / ramp_s))
            command = desired * fraction
        data.ctrl[0] = command
        mujoco.mj_step(model, data)
        rows.append((float(data.time), desired, command,
                     float(data.qpos[0]), float(data.qvel[0])))
    rms_error = math.sqrt(sum((r[1] - r[3]) ** 2 for r in rows) / len(rows))
    peak_speed = max(abs(r[4]) for r in rows)
    # A teaching objective: angle error plus a preference for lower peak speed.
    cost = rms_error + SPEED_WEIGHT_S * peak_speed
    return {"cost": cost, "rms_error_rad": rms_error,
            "peak_speed_rad_s": peak_speed, "final_rad": rows[-1][3]}, rows


def save_trace(stem, rows):
    stem = Path(stem)
    with stem.with_suffix(".csv").open("w", newline="") as f:
        writer = csv.writer(f)
        writer.writerow(["time_s", "desired_rad", "command_rad", "position_rad", "speed_rad_s"])
        writer.writerows(rows)
    lines = []
    for column, color in ((1, "#a65b35"), (2, "#8b7c25"), (3, "#205e4a")):
        points = " ".join(f"{60+r[0]*310:.2f},{260-r[column]*400:.2f}" for r in rows[::5])
        lines.append(f'<polyline points="{points}" fill="none" stroke="{color}" stroke-width="2"/>')
    svg = '<svg xmlns="http://www.w3.org/2000/svg" viewBox="0 0 740 320" role="img">'
    svg += '<title>Joint response: desired, commanded, and measured angle</title>'
    svg += '<rect width="740" height="320" fill="#faf7ed"/>'
    svg += '<path d="M60 40V260H680" stroke="#555" fill="none"/>'
    svg += '<g font-family="sans-serif" font-size="13" fill="#333">'
    svg += '<text x="60" y="24">Joint angle / radians</text><text x="43" y="264">0</text>'
    svg += '<text x="30" y="104">0.4</text><text x="60" y="282">0 s</text><text x="660" y="282">2 s</text>'
    svg += '<text x="75" y="307" fill="#a65b35">Desired</text><text x="170" y="307" fill="#8b7c25">Command</text>'
    svg += '<text x="280" y="307" fill="#205e4a">Measured in simulation</text></g>'
    svg += "".join(lines) + "</svg>"
    stem.with_suffix(".svg").write_text(svg, encoding="utf-8")


def main():
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser(description=__doc__)
    parser.add_argument("--ramp", type=float, default=0.0, help="command rise time in seconds")
    parser.add_argument("--mass", type=float, default=0.10, help="simulated link mass in kg")
    parser.add_argument("--target-deg", type=float, default=math.degrees(TARGET), help="requested angle change in degrees")
    parser.add_argument("--out", default="baseline", help="output filename stem")
    args = parser.parse_args()
    metrics, rows = run_trial(args.ramp, args.mass, math.radians(args.target_deg))
    save_trace(args.out, rows)
    print(f"MuJoCo {mujoco.__version__}; ramp={args.ramp:.3f}s; mass={args.mass:.3f}kg")
    for name, value in metrics.items():
        print(f"{name}: {value:.6f}")
    print(f"Wrote {args.out}.csv and {args.out}.svg")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
Under the lid: why the program has two clocks

The physics advances every 0.002 seconds: 500 steps per simulated second. The command updates every ten physics steps: 50 times per second. It stays unchanged between updates. A controller can run more slowly than its physics model; the important part is making that timing explicit. This program runs simulated time as quickly as the computer permits. Its two-second trial does not need to take two wall-clock seconds.

The CSV includes desired angle, command, resulting angle, and angular speed. Only the last two come from the simulated state. The real SG92R gives you no equivalent built-in position measurement through its three wires.

1. Decide what “better” means

For this exercise, better means a compromise between staying near the desired angle and avoiding high peak angular speed. We score every two-second trial using:

cost = RMS angle error + 0.03 seconds × peak angular speed

Lower cost wins. RMS means root mean square: square each error, average those squares, then take the square root. It gives one number for how far the joint is from its desired angle across the trial. Both terms in our cost have angle units; the 0.03-second weight expresses a preference we chose. It is not a material property, an energy measurement, or a universal safety limit.

The baseline jumps to the target immediately. Candidates take between 0.02 and 0.60 seconds to ramp up. Every candidate gets the same target, joint model, torque limit, starting position, and trial duration. Otherwise we could accidentally reward an easier test.

"""Search command rise times; evaluate the chosen setting on unseen link masses."""
import csv
import json
import random
from joint_probe import run_trial, save_trace

SEED = 7
TRAIN_MASS_KG = 0.10
HELD_OUT_MASSES_KG = (0.08, 0.12)  # decided before searching


def main():
    rng = random.Random(SEED)
    candidates = [0.0] + [rng.uniform(0.02, 0.60) for _ in range(24)]
    results = []
    for ramp in candidates:
        metrics, _ = run_trial(ramp, TRAIN_MASS_KG)
        results.append({"ramp_s": ramp, **metrics})
    best = min(results, key=lambda item: item["cost"])

Parameters are the numbers training fits, such as regression coefficients or network weights. A loss measures disagreement between predictions and examples; training adjusts parameters to reduce it. Start with three input values, two hidden layers of 32 units with ReLU activations, and two output values. These are a deliberately small first architecture, not a claim that it is optimal.
  2. Reuse the frozen dataset and training-only scaling. Train on mean squared error of the standardized two targets so radians and radians/second are not mixed without a declared scale. Convert predictions back to physical units for every reported metric.
  3. Start with Adam at a learning rate of 0.001, batches of 256, and at most 100 epochs. An epoch is one pass over the training examples. Save the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss; stop after ten epochs without improvement. These are starting choices. If you change them, log the reason and use validation results, not the final test, to choose.
  4. First check that a tiny subset of training examples can be fitted closely. This is a debugging check for shapes, target alignment, and the update loop, not a generalization result. Then train on the full training split and plot training versus validation loss. Run the fixed recipe with three recorded initialization seeds; retain every result.

Let the agent write loaders, plots, and the training loop from your contract. You own the feature/target definition, split boundaries, loss, and interpretation. Ask it to walk through the shapes and units of one batch and to show which values change during one optimizer step. That review teaches more than accepting an impressive-looking loss curve.

Pass when: you can reload the weights and preprocessing state and reproduce predictions with training updates disabled. Follow PyTorch’s save/load example. Save code, package versions, seeds, split manifest, scalers, checkpoint, and curves. Next: freeze the recipe and open the test split.

Session 10E · Find out where the model stops being useful

60–90 minutes · Finish with a comparison and a stated limit

  1. Run the frozen baselines and network recipe on the untouched nominal-mass test episodes. Report per-output RMSE, motion-window error, and per-episode results; report all three training seeds rather than presenting only the best. A network that does not beat the simpler model is a valid result.
  2. Run the mass-change stress tests separately. This predictor has no mass input and learned at one mass; do not assume the same observed state and command identify all the changed physics. Label this as a change of conditions, not another sample from the training setup.
  3. On a test command sequence, start from its true initial state. Convert each predicted change into the next state: q_next = q + predicted Δq and v_next = v + predicted Δv. Feed that predicted state back into the model for one second. Use the recorded upcoming commands, but do not replace intermediate predictions with simulator truth. Plot angle and velocity against the simulator. Small one-step errors can accumulate into an unusable rollout.
  4. Write a short model report: task, data coverage, baselines, split policy, units, errors, failure cases, and what the model is not suitable for. If test results lead you to redesign the model, those results have become development feedback; reserve new final-test episodes for the next comparison.

Finish line: a fitted model, reproducible inference, and evidence of where it helps or fails. Predictive accuracy alone does not make it a controller or establish safe real-world behavior. For your crawler, a candidate can contain wave amplitude, frequency, phase offset, and turning bias. The score can use measured forward distance, unwanted heading change, and failed runs. Optimization by itself does not create feedback. A selected fixed wave remains open-loop: it will keep executing even if the floor changes. A controller that adjusts a wave from actual sensor measurements is a separate capability.

The next expedition: a crawler that reacts

Your long-term operator request can be “forward speed plus turn rate.” A policy chooses bounded gait adjustments from that request and observations. A neural network can represent that policy; reinforcement learning can fit it through rewards from interaction. This is a new job compared with predicting the joint’s next state. A genetic algorithm instead searches candidate parameters using selection and variation. Adversarial imitation can supply a motion-style objective when suitable reference motion exists; building a general-purpose GAN is not a prerequisite for locomotion.

Keep the execution boundary explicit: operator request + available observations → policy → local angle/rate limits and command timeout → actuators. Limits and the watchdog stay outside the learned model. A stalled laptop or invalid prediction must still reach the defined stopping behavior.

Keep this order for the next project:

  1. Measure one real joint. Record its response at the allowed loads and command rate. Ordinary SG92R commands are not measured joint angles.
  2. Build and check the whole crawler model. Match hinge axes, mass, actuator response, and passive-wheel contact. A chain of boxes on a generic floor is not the same mechanism. Replay one known gait and compare direction and displacement with the physical trials.
  3. Search the measured gait parameters. Reuse the baseline, reserved conditions, and results log you practiced here. Keep the physical angle/rate bounds.
  4. Add a measured heading signal and a simple corrective controller. Confirm that a turning request has a predictable effect before training a control policy.

A small working vocabulary
Supervised learning
Fitting model parameters from examples that include both inputs and a desired prediction target.
Feature and target
Features are the inputs available when making a prediction. The target is the value the model is trained to predict; later measurements cannot be smuggled into current inputs.
Loss
A numeric measure of prediction error used to fit model parameters. Its scale, units, and weighting affect what the fit prioritizes.
Training, validation, and test
Training data fits parameters and preprocessing. Validation data guides model choices. A reserved test set evaluates the frozen choice; using it to guide revisions makes it part of development.
Gradient
The local sensitivity of a loss to model parameters. An optimizer uses this information to choose parameter updates.
Training and inference
Training adjusts fitted parameters using data. Inference applies the fitted model to new inputs; it does not automatically continue learning.
Data leakage
Information that should be unavailable during fitting or prediction influences the model or its evaluation, producing misleadingly optimistic results.
Clearance
Intentional space between mating parts. A pocket with clearance on both sides adds twice the per-side clearance to the part width.
Tolerance
The permitted variation in a dimension. It describes an acceptance range, not a universal printer capability.
Dimensional accuracy
In this workshop, how closely a printed feature matches its intended dimension. A fine layer height or precise-looking display does not establish it.
Repeatability
How closely repeated measurements or prints agree under the same stated conditions. Consistent results can still have a consistent error.
Diametral and radial clearance
For centered circular parts, diametral clearance is hole diameter minus shaft diameter; radial clearance is half that difference.
Fit coupon
A small test piece used to check a critical fit before printing the full part.
Actuator
A component that turns a command and an energy supply into physical action, such as a motor.
Backlash
Free motion caused by clearance in a mechanism. The output can lag when you reverse direction.
Closed-loop control
A controller uses measurements of what happened to adjust what it commands next.
Command
A requested value. It is not evidence that the mechanism reached that value.
Degree of freedom
One independent coordinate needed to describe a mechanism’s configuration.
Duty cycle
The fraction of a PWM cycle spent in the active state. Its relationship to motor motion depends on the driver, motor, load, and supply.
Encoder
A sensor that measures rotation or displacement; wheel rotation can differ from ground travel when the wheel slips.
Gait
A coordinated pattern of movement that can produce locomotion when the body interacts appropriately with its surroundings.
Ground / common reference
The electrical reference shared by communicating circuits. It does not mean every positive supply rail should be connected together.
Kinematics
The geometry of motion. It relates configurations, velocities, and positions without describing the forces that cause them.
Dynamics
The relationship between forces, torques, mass, and the resulting motion.
Observation
Information available to a controller or policy. It may be incomplete or noisy.
Open-loop control
Commands are generated without correction from measurements of the resulting behavior.
Policy
A rule that selects actions from observations and, in our later experiments, a requested motion.
Reward
A training signal expressing what behavior is preferred. An imperfect reward can favor unwanted behavior.
System identification
Using measurements to estimate how a physical system behaves and to improve its model.
Domain randomization
Varying plausible simulated conditions during training to reduce dependence on one exact simulated world.
Torque
The turning effect of a force about an axis. For a perpendicular force, its magnitude is force multiplied by the lever arm.
Watchdog / command timeout
A local check that changes to a defined stopping behavior when fresh commands stop arriving.
